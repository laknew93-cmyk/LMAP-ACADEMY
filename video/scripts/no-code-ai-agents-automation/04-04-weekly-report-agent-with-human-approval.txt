Welcome to No-Code AI Agents and Automation, module 4, lesson 4: Project 4, Weekly Report Agent with Human Approval.

In this lesson, you'll build Project 4. It's an agent that gathers data from several sources every week, writes a report, and then waits for a human to approve, edit or reject it before anything gets sent. Human-in-the-loop design is what makes AI automation safe enough for real businesses.

So why human-in-the-loop? Some outputs are low-risk, like a label on an email or a row in a sheet. Others carry your name, or your client's name. A report to investors. A message to five hundred customers. A payment. For those, the pattern is simple. AI does the work, a human approves, and automation delivers.

You keep most of the time savings, because reviewing a draft takes minutes, while writing it takes hours. And a person stays accountable for what goes out.

Here's the scenario. Nikhil is the founder of a D to C spice brand in Hyderabad. Every Monday, his ops manager, Fatima, spends two hours pulling numbers from the order sheet, the ad spend sheet and the support inbox. Then she writes a summary for Nikhil and two investors. The goal is for Fatima to review a ready draft on Monday morning, in ten minutes.

Let's walk through the workflow design. The full outline is in the lesson notes. Step one, a schedule trigger, every Monday at seven a m. Step two, an agent step with four read-only tools. Get weekly orders, from the orders sheet for the last seven days. Get ad spend, from the ads sheet for the last seven days. Get support summary, from the triage log you built in Project 2. And get last week's report, for comparison. Step three, the agent writes the report draft in a fixed template. Step four, save the draft to a Google Doc, and log a row with the status "PENDING". Step five, send an approval request to Fatima, with approve, edit and reject options. Step six, wait for the response. Step seven depends on her answer. If she approves, send the report email to Nikhil and the investors, and set the status to "SENT". If she edits, she changes the Doc, then approves, and the edited version gets sent. If she rejects, the status becomes "REJECTED", Fatima's notified, and nothing is sent. And step eight is a timeout. If there's no response by twelve noon, send a reminder. By the end of the day, mark it "EXPIRED".

Now, why use an agent here, and not just a fixed workflow? Honestly, the data gathering could be fixed steps. The agent earns its place in the analysis. It decides what to look into further. If orders dropped, it can check whether ad spend also dropped, or whether support complaints about delivery went up. Giving it tools, rather than pre-computed tables, lets it follow the thread. But if your report is purely numbers, a fixed workflow with one AI summary step is simpler. And that's a perfectly good choice.

Next, the agent instructions. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. It tells the agent to prepare the weekly business report for Nikhil Spices, using the tools to get data for the seven days ending yesterday, and to compare with last week's report. It sets an exact six-part structure. A one-sentence headline. Key numbers, meaning orders, revenue in rupees, average order value, ad spend, and the revenue-to-ad-spend ratio, this week versus last week. What went well, in up to three points. What needs attention, in up to three points. Customer support themes, in up to three points. And up to two questions for the team. Then come the rules. Every number must come from a tool result, and never estimate or round unless you say so. If a tool fails or the data looks incomplete, say so clearly at the top. Separate facts from interpretation, and label guesses as "possible reason". And no hype. Just plain, direct business English.

Those rules, that every number must come from a tool, and to say so if data's missing, are critical. A report that confidently invents revenue figures is worse than no report at all.

And consider doing the maths outside the AI. Language models can make arithmetic mistakes, especially with lots of rows. A sturdy pattern is to compute totals and percentages in fixed steps, using sheet formulas or a summarise or aggregate step, and then give the agent the computed numbers. Let the AI do what it's good at, which is spotting patterns and writing, and let formulas do the arithmetic.

Now, building the approval step. All three major tools support pausing for a human, but with different mechanics. There are four options. First, wait-for-response nodes. n eight n can send a message, by email, Slack, Telegram and others, with approval buttons, and pause the workflow until someone responds. It also supports form-based and webhook-based waits. Second, approval links. A common no-code pattern is an email with two links, approve and reject, each pointing to a webhook U R L with the report I D. Clicking one starts, or resumes, the next workflow. Third, status-column approval, the simplest of all. The workflow writes "PENDING" in a sheet. Fatima changes it to "APPROVED". And a second workflow, triggered by that change, or checking every fifteen minutes, sends the report. And fourth, built-in approval features. Some platforms offer native human-in-the-loop or approval steps, so check your tool's current options.

The status-column method is less elegant, but it's extremely robust, and easy for clients to understand. If you're unsure, start there.

Next, approval message design. Make approving easy and informed. The lesson's example message says the weekly report is ready for review for the week ending twelfth October. It shows the headline: "Orders up, but ad efficiency dropped, and delivery complaints rose in Pune." It says there are no data warnings. It links to the Google Doc to review and edit. And it offers approve or reject. Show the headline and any data warnings in the message itself, so the reviewer knows where to look.

And there are four safety rules for approvals. One, send exactly what was approved. If Fatima edits the Doc, the send step must read the latest Doc content, not the original draft. Two, one approval, one send. Mark the row "SENT" immediately, and check it before sending, so a double-click doesn't email the investors twice. Three, approvals expire. A report approved three weeks late shouldn't go out silently. And four, only the right person can approve. Use unguessable I Ds in approval links, or restrict who can edit the status column.

Now, try it yourself. Build Project 4 in about twenty minutes, using sample data. Pause the video and work along. Step one, create a sheet with fourteen days of fake orders, with the date, order I D, amount in rupees, and city, plus a small ad-spend sheet. Step two, build a scheduled workflow, and run it manually for testing, with an agent step that has read-only tools for both sheets. Optionally, compute the totals with fixed steps first. Step three, use the report template instructions from this lesson. Step four, save the draft to a Google Doc, and add a log row with the status "PENDING". Step five, implement approval using the status-column method. A second workflow sends the Doc content to your email only when the status becomes "APPROVED", and then sets it to "SENT". And step six, test approve, reject, and a double-approval attempt.

Let's recap. Human-in-the-loop means AI drafts, a person approves, and automation delivers. Use agents for analysis that benefits from following leads, and use fixed steps or formulas for the arithmetic. Require every number to come from data, and surface data problems at the top. Approval can be buttons, links, or a simple status column, and robustness beats elegance. And send exactly what was approved, exactly once, and let stale approvals expire.
