Welcome to No-Code AI Agents and Automation, module 5, lesson 3: Scoping, Pricing and Maintaining Client Work.

Getting a "yes" from a client is half the job. The other half is scoping clearly, pricing sensibly, handing over properly, and earning ongoing income from maintenance. This final lesson gives you the templates and habits to run automation projects professionally.

Let's start with scoping tightly. Most automation projects that go badly were scoped vaguely. "Automate our sales process" has no end. But compare that to this: "When a lead submits the website form, score it with AI, save it to the Leads sheet, alert the owner on WhatsApp for hot leads, and send an approved WhatsApp template within five minutes." That has a clear finish line.

A good scope document covers ten sections, and the full template is in the lesson notes below the video. One, the goal, meaning the business outcome in one sentence. Two, what's in scope: each workflow, its trigger, its steps, and its outputs. Three, what's out of scope, meaning what you're not building. Be explicit. Four, what the client provides. That's account access, an AI provider account and key, a WhatsApp provider account, sample data, and a contact person. Five, tools and running costs: which platforms, and who pays for them. Six, data handling: what data flows where, and which AI provider processes it. Seven, testing and acceptance: the test set, and what "done" means. Eight, the timeline, with milestones and dates. Nine, handover, meaning documentation and a walkthrough session. And ten, support: what's included after launch, and for how long.

The "out of scope" and "client provides" sections prevent most disputes. And delays usually come from waiting for access, not from building.

Next, pricing approaches. There's no standard price for automation work. Rates vary widely by city, niche, experience and client size. But there are four common structures, and there's a table of them in the lesson notes. First, a fixed project fee, which is one price for a scoped build. That's good for clear, well-defined workflows. Second, setup plus a monthly retainer. You charge a build fee, and then a monthly fee for monitoring, fixes and small changes. That's good for most small business clients, and it creates recurring income. Third, an hourly or daily rate, where you bill for time spent. That's good for discovery, audits, or unclear scope. And fourth, value-based pricing, where the price is tied to the value created. That's for experienced builders with proven results.

Now, thinking about the number. Rather than copying someone else's rate card, reason it out from three angles. First, your time. Estimate the build, testing, meetings and handover hours honestly, and then add a buffer, because access and revisions always take longer. Second, the value to the client. If the workflow saves a team many hours a month, or speeds up lead response, your fee should feel small next to that value. And third, the client's size. A three-person clinic and a two-hundred-person manufacturer can afford different amounts for the same workflow.

Purely as an illustration of structure, and not as a benchmark: a beginner might quote a modest fixed fee for a single lead workflow, plus a small monthly retainer. An experienced builder with case studies might charge several times that for a multi-workflow system. Your rates will depend on your market and your results. Raise them as your proof grows.

Remember that running costs are separate. Make it clear, in writing, that tool subscriptions, AI usage and WhatsApp messaging charges are paid by the client directly to the providers, or itemised if you pass them through. And mention that these costs can vary with volume and with provider pricing changes.

What about G S T and invoicing? If you're registered under G S T, your invoices need to follow G S T rules. Whether you need to register depends on your turnover and the type of supply. Speak to a C A about your own situation, and use a simple written agreement for every client.

Next, payment terms. Common, fair practice for project work is an advance before starting, often a portion like half, with the balance on acceptance. Retainers are billed monthly, in advance. Accept U P I or bank transfer, and send a proper invoice each time. And don't start building before the advance lands. It protects both of you from casual commitments.

Now, handover done right. At the end of the project, deliver four things. First, the one-page workflow document for each workflow, from the previous lesson. Second, a short screen-recorded walkthrough, showing what it does, where to see the logs, and what to do if an alert arrives. Third, ownership confirmation. The workflows live in the client's account, or a clearly agreed account. All credentials are in the client's name. And you have access only as a team member they can remove. And fourth, the test set, so future changes can be checked.

Building inside the client's own accounts from day one makes handover trivial, and it builds trust. Avoid locking clients in by keeping their workflows in your personal account. It might feel like leverage, but it damages your reputation.

Next, maintenance and retainers. Workflows need care. Apps update, forms change, AI models get retired and replaced, and prompts need tuning as the business changes. A monthly retainer can include four things. One, monitoring alerts and fixing failures within an agreed response time. Two, a monthly health check, reviewing error logs, AI quality samples, and costs. Three, a fixed number of small changes per month, for example a new label or a new form field. And four, a short monthly summary for the owner, covering runs, errors, items needing review, and suggestions.

Define response times realistically. "Within one working day" is more honest than "twenty-four seven" for a solo freelancer.

Now, growing beyond the first project. The best source of your next project is your current client. After a successful lead workflow, the natural next steps are often an FAQ bot, inbox triage, or a weekly report. Use the monthly summary to suggest one improvement at a time. And ask satisfied clients for a referral, or a short written testimonial in their own words.

Finally, ethics and trust. There are four principles that keep you in business for the long term. First, tell end customers when they're talking to a bot, and offer them a human. Second, never automate spam, scraped contact lists, or messages without consent. Third, keep humans in control of money, legal and medical decisions. And fourth, be honest about what AI can't do. Under-promise, and over-deliver.

Now, try it yourself. Spend about twenty minutes preparing your first proposal. Pause the video and work through these steps. Step one, pick one project you built in this course, and imagine a specific client for it. Step two, fill in the ten-section scope template from this lesson. Step three, decide on a pricing model, and write a short justification based on your estimated hours and the client's likely value. Step four, draft a one-paragraph retainer offer listing what's included each month. And step five, ask a friend who runs a business to read it, and tell you what's unclear.

Let's recap. A tight scope, with explicit "out of scope" and "client provides" sections, prevents most disputes. Choose a pricing model, whether fixed, setup plus retainer, hourly, or value-based, and reason from your time, the value, and the client's size. Running costs belong to the client, and take advice from a C A on G S T and invoicing. Build in the client's accounts, and hand over documentation, a walkthrough, and the test set. And maintenance retainers create recurring income, and keep workflows healthy.
