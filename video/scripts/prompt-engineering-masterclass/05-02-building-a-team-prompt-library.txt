Welcome to Prompt Engineering Masterclass, module 5, lesson 2: Building a Team Prompt Library.

A single great prompt helps you. But a well-organised library of tested prompts helps your entire team. And it's one of the most convincing portfolio pieces you can show. In this lesson, you'll assemble your twenty-five prompt professional library, which is the main course project.

So, why does a library beat scattered prompts? In most teams, good prompts live in one person's chat history. When that person is on leave, quality drops. A shared library gives you four things. First, consistency. Client emails, reports and posts all follow the same standard. Second, speed. New joiners are productive on day one. Third, improvement. Everyone's fixes flow back into one place. And fourth, evidence. You can show managers and interviewers exactly how you use AI.

Next, choose a home for it. Start with what your team already uses. That could be a Google Sheet, a Notion database, a Confluence page, a SharePoint list, or even a well-structured Google Doc. A spreadsheet or database works best, because you can filter and sort. Some teams later move templates into custom assistants or internal tools, but the library remains the source of truth.

Now, the library entry format. Every prompt gets the same fields. Let me walk you through the example in the notes. It has an I D, M K T dash zero seven, and a name, "Blog-to-social repurposer". The category is Marketing, then Content. The "Use when" line says you have a published blog, and need LinkedIn, Instagram and WhatsApp versions. The inputs needed are the blog text, the target audience, and one link. Then comes the full prompt template, with variables in square brackets and tags for pasted content. After that, a short, anonymised example input, and a short excerpt of example output. The "Tested" field says eight cases, seven out of eight passing, on version three, with a weakness on very technical blogs. The tools tried were ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini, and all were fine. The owner is Ritu S. It was last updated in October 2026. The version notes say version three added a banned-words list. And the safety notes say, don't paste unpublished client strategy. You'll find the full entry in the lesson notes below this video.

Here's the thing. The "Tested", "Owner" and "Last updated" fields are what make it professional. They tell a teammate whether to trust the prompt.

Let's talk about organising twenty-five prompts. Group them by job, not by technique. There's a table in the lesson notes with a common structure for a knowledge worker: five categories, with five prompts each. Communication covers things like a leave request, a client follow-up, a difficult conversation, a payment reminder, and a meeting invite. Writing and content covers a style-matched post, a blog outline, a repurposer, a proofreader, and headline variations. Analysis and research covers a decision summary, review themes, a vendor comparison, grounded Q and A, and an assumption checker. Data and productivity covers an extraction schema, notes-to-actions, a checklist builder, a formula helper, and a SQL helper. And thinking and quality covers critique-and-rewrite, a role-switch review, red-teaming a plan, a meeting report chain, and a prompt improver.

Adapt the categories to your role. A teacher might have lesson planning, assessment, parent communication, differentiation and admin. A sales professional might have prospecting, discovery, proposals, follow-up and CRM hygiene.

Next, a prompt that improves prompts. Every library should include a meta-prompt that helps teammates improve their own drafts. The example in the notes sets the model up as a prompt engineering reviewer, and gives it four steps. First, identify what's missing: role, task, context, constraints, format, examples, and handling of missing information. Second, ask up to three questions if critical information is missing. Third, produce an improved version, using labelled sections, variables in square brackets for anything that changes between uses, and tags for pasted content. And fourth, list three test inputs to try it on, including one edge case. Then you paste your draft prompt inside draft prompt tags. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

Now, naming and versioning. There are four simple habits. First, use short I Ds by category, like C O M zero one, W R I zero four, or A N A zero two. Second, use a simple version number, like v1, v2, v3, with a one-line change note. Third, don't overwrite silently. If a change makes things worse, you need to be able to go back. And fourth, archive prompts nobody has used in months, rather than deleting them.

Next, a quality gate for team prompts. Before a prompt enters the shared library, it should pass a short checklist with six checks. One, it has a clear name and a "use when" line. Two, all variables are in capitals, and pasted content is in tags. Three, it defines what to do when information is missing. Four, it's been tested on at least five inputs, with the results recorded. Five, there's no confidential data inside the template itself. And six, an owner is assigned. The checklist is in the lesson notes below the video.

Now, rolling it out to a team. There are five steps. First, start small. Ten excellent prompts beat a hundred untested ones. Second, demo, don't decree. Show a before and after in a fifteen-minute team meeting. Third, collect feedback. Add a "Suggestions" column, or a channel for comments. Fourth, review monthly. Update prompts when tools, policies or products change. And fifth, celebrate contributions. Credit owners by name. People improve what they feel ownership of.

Finally, using it on your CV and LinkedIn. A library is concrete evidence of prompt engineering skill. Describe it honestly and specifically. For example: "Built and tested a twenty-five prompt library for marketing workflows, covering content, analysis and reporting, with documented test cases and version history." If you're asked, share a sanitised sample of three to five entries. Never client data.

Now it's time to try it yourself. This is the project: your twenty-five prompt professional library. It spans several sessions, so start with twenty minutes today. Pause the video and work through these steps. Step one. Create a spreadsheet with the entry fields we covered as columns. Step two. Add the prompts you've saved from earlier lessons. You should have eight to twelve. Step three. Decide your five categories based on your role, and list the gaps. Step four. Today, write and quick-test three new prompts to fill the biggest gaps. Step five. Over the next week, complete all twenty-five, each tested on at least three inputs, with your five most-used prompts tested on five or more. And step six. Run your weakest prompt through the meta-prompt, and log the improvement.

Let's recap. A shared, tested prompt library gives teams consistency, speed and continuous improvement. Every entry needs a name, a use case, inputs, the template, test results, an owner and a date. Organise prompts by job category, not by technique. Version your prompts, never overwrite silently, and gate new entries with a quality checklist. And a sanitised library is strong, honest evidence of prompt engineering skill for your CV.
