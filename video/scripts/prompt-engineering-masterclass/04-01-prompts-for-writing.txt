Welcome to Prompt Engineering Masterclass, module 4, lesson 1: Prompts for Writing That Sounds Like You.

Writing is where most people first use AI. It's also where generic output is most obvious. In this lesson, you'll get prompts that produce writing in your voice, for a specific reader, with a clear purpose.

Let's start with the three things every writing prompt needs. First, the reader. Who exactly will read this, and what do they already know? Second, the purpose. What should the reader think, feel or do afterwards? And third, the voice. How should it sound? Ideally, you show this with a sample. Here's the thing. Most bland AI writing is missing at least two of these.

Let's look at emails. Here's a typical "before" prompt: "Write an email asking my manager for leave." Now the "after" version. It asks for a short email to the manager, Suresh, requesting leave. Then it adds a facts block. The dates are the twelfth to the sixteenth of November, five working days, for a sister's wedding in Madurai. The open work is the monthly vendor reconciliation, due on the tenth of November, which will be finished early. And the backup is Divya, who's agreed to handle urgent vendor queries. Then the prompt states the purpose: get approval without back-and-forth. The tone: polite, confident, not over-apologetic, and the office uses first names. And the length: under a hundred words, with a subject line. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. The facts block makes the email complete. The purpose line makes it decisive.

Next, matching your own voice. Paste two or three samples of your own writing, and ask the model to analyse them first. The lesson's example uses three LinkedIn posts that performed well. The prompt asks the model to first describe the writing style in five bullet points, covering sentence length, vocabulary, how you open, how you close, and your use of humour or emojis. Then it asks for a new post in exactly that style, on your chosen topic. The posts go inside tags, separated by dividers. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. And here's a bonus. The style description is useful on its own. Save it, and add it to future writing prompts as "My style guide".

Now, rewriting and editing. Editing prompts work best when you say what kind of edit you want. The lesson notes have a table of five edit types, each with a ready-made phrase. For a light proofread, say: "Fix grammar and spelling only. Do not change wording or tone." For a clarity edit, ask it to simplify sentences for a Class 8 reading level and keep all facts. To shorten, ask it to cut to a hundred and fifty words, keeping the three main points and the call to action. For a tone shift, ask it to make the text firmer but still polite, because it's your second payment reminder. And to localise, ask it to adapt the text for small business owners in Tier-2 cities, using rupees and familiar examples.

When it matters, ask to see the changes. Here's the prompt: "Edit the paragraph below for clarity. Then list each change you made and why, so I can accept or reject them."

Let's move on to long-form content. For blogs, reports and articles, don't ask for the full piece in one go. Use a mini-chain with four steps. First, the angle. For example: "Suggest five angles for a blog on G S T for freelance designers. For each, give the reader's main question." Second, the outline. Ask for a detailed outline for, say, angle three, with section headings, key points under each, and where an example would help. Third, draft section by section. For example: "Write section two only, two hundred and fifty words, using this real example." And fourth, polish. Ask for an intro hook and a conclusion with a clear next step. That way, you stay in control of the argument, and the AI does the heavy drafting.

Next, writing for different formats. The same content needs different shapes. The lesson's repurposing prompt takes a blog post and turns it into four things. First, a LinkedIn post, under two hundred words, with a hook in the first line and no hashtag spam. Second, an Instagram carousel outline of seven slides, with a maximum of fifteen words per slide. Third, a WhatsApp broadcast message for existing clients, under sixty words, with one link. And fourth, three tweet-length takeaways. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. This repurposing prompt is one of the highest-value prompts for marketers and small business owners.

What about teachers and trainers? The lesson includes a prompt where the model plays an experienced Class 7 science teacher in a C B S E school. It creates a forty-minute lesson plan on "Acids, Bases and Salts". The plan includes learning objectives, a five-minute hook using household items you'd find in an Indian kitchen, one group activity, and five exit-ticket questions: two easy, two medium and one challenging. It also adds a two-line note for students who were absent, and it's formatted with clear headings and timings. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

Now, avoiding the "AI voice". Readers are getting better at spotting AI-sounding text. Think over-enthusiastic openers, words like "delve" and "tapestry", phrases like "in today's fast-paced world", tidy groups of three, and empty conclusions. So add a banned list to your style guide. Here's the prompt: "Avoid: In today's fast-paced world, delve, unlock, game-changer, elevate, seamless, rhetorical questions as openers, and ending with a generic summary. Prefer concrete details and plain verbs." And always do a final read yourself. Your name is on it.

Now it's your turn. Spend fifteen to twenty minutes, and pause the video as you go. Step one. Collect two or three pieces of your own writing, like posts, emails, or a report section. Step two. Use the voice-analysis prompt to generate your five-bullet style guide, then add a banned-words list. Step three. Write one real email using the reader, purpose, facts and tone structure. Step four. Take any long piece, either yours or a public article, and run the repurposing prompt for three formats. And step five. Save your style guide and your two best prompts to your library.

Let's recap. Every writing prompt needs a reader, a purpose and a voice. Put facts in a separate block, so drafts are complete and accurate. Build a personal style guide from your own samples, and reuse it. Write long-form content as a chain: angle, outline, section drafts, then polish. And ban AI cliches explicitly, and always do the final edit yourself.
