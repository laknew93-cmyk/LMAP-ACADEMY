Welcome to Prompt Engineering Masterclass, module 4, lesson 4: Image Generation Prompting Basics.

Many chat assistants can now generate images, and dedicated image tools are widely used for marketing, presentations and social media. But image prompts work differently from text prompts. In this lesson, I'll give you a reliable structure, and walk you through the main pitfalls to avoid.

So, how do image prompts differ? Text prompts are instructions. Image prompts are closer to descriptions of a picture that should exist. The model needs to know what's in the scene, how it looks, and how it's framed. Vague prompts give you stock-photo-style results. Specific visual detail gives you distinctive ones.

Let's look at the image prompt structure. There are eight elements to cover, roughly in this order. First, the subject. Who or what is the focus? Second, the action or pose. What's happening? Third, the setting. Where is it, and what background details matter? Fourth, the style. Is it a photo, a watercolour, a flat vector illustration, a 3D render, or line art? Fifth, the composition. A close-up, a wide shot, a top-down view, the rule of thirds, or space left for text. Sixth, lighting and colour. Think soft morning light, warm tones, or your brand colours. Seventh, the mood. Calm, festive, professional, or playful. And eighth, the format. That's the aspect ratio, where the tool supports it. Square for an Instagram feed, vertical for stories, and wide for slides.

Let's see a before and after. Here's the before prompt: "Picture of a bakery for Instagram."

Now the after. Here's the prompt: "A warm, realistic photo of a small neighbourhood bakery counter in Pune in the early morning. A glass display holds nankhatai, rusk and a jaggery millet cake on a wooden stand. Soft golden sunlight through the window, steam rising from a cup of cutting chai on the counter. Shallow depth of field, focus on the cake. Earthy browns and cream tones. Calm, homely mood. Square format, with empty space at the top for text."

That second prompt gives you a usable, on-brand image far more often.

Next, style vocabulary that works. There's a handy table in the lesson notes that matches what you want to the words that get it. For a realistic photo, try words like "photograph", "natural light", "shallow depth of field" and "35 millimetre". For a clean marketing graphic, try "flat vector illustration", "minimal" and "solid background". For a friendly explainer, use "simple cartoon illustration", "rounded shapes" and "pastel palette". For a premium product, use "studio product shot", "soft shadows" and "white seamless background". And for a traditional Indian art feel, try "inspired by Madhubani folk art patterns" or "Warli-style line figures".

One important point here. Describe styles and movements, rather than naming living artists. Copying a specific living artist's style raises ethical, and sometimes legal, questions. And many tools restrict it anyway.

Now, text in images. Image models have improved at rendering text, but it's still a common failure point, especially for long phrases or non-Latin scripts. So here's a practical approach, in two parts. First, keep any in-image text very short, just one to four words, and put it in quotes. For example, a sign reading "OPEN". Second, for posters, flyers and social posts, generate the image with empty space, and add the text yourself in Canva, PowerPoint or a similar tool. That way you get correct spelling, your brand fonts, and easy edits.

Let's talk about iterating on images. Image work is highly iterative, so change one thing at a time. Here's the prompt: "Same image, but make it evening with warm string lights, and move the cake to the left third of the frame."

If a result is close, many tools let you edit a selected region, or ask for variations. And save the prompts that produce good results, along with the image, so you can recreate the look later.

Here's a neat trick. Use a text assistant to write your image prompts for you. The example prompt in the notes asks a chat assistant for a hero image for a LinkedIn post, announcing a free G S T filing webinar for small shop owners in Tier-2 cities. It asks for three detailed image-generation prompts in different styles: a photo, a flat illustration, and a minimal 3D style. Each one should include subject, setting, composition, lighting, and colours, with the brand being teal and white. Each should leave space at the top for a headline, and have no text inside the image. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

Now, responsible image use. There are five things to keep in mind. First, real people. Don't generate realistic images of real, identifiable people without consent, and never in misleading or harmful situations. Many tools block this anyway. Second, disclosure. For news-like or documentary contexts, don't present AI images as real photographs. Follow your platform's and your employer's labelling rules. Third, products. Don't use AI images that misrepresent what a customer will actually receive. For example, a Jaipur jewellery seller should photograph the actual necklace for the listing, and use AI for lifestyle backgrounds or concept visuals. Fourth, rights. Check your tool's terms on commercial use, and your company's policy, before using images in paid ads or client work. And fifth, bias. Image models can default to stereotypes. So specify diversity deliberately where it's relevant: age, gender, skin tone, region and clothing.

Now it's time to try it yourself. Spend fifteen minutes, using any image tool you have access to. Pause the video whenever you need to. Step one. Choose a real need, like a social post, a slide visual, a blog header, or a classroom illustration. Step two. Write a one-line prompt, and generate an image. Step three. Rewrite it with all eight elements: subject, action, setting, style, composition, lighting, mood and format. Then generate again. Step four. Make two single-change iterations. Step five. Ask a chat assistant to write three alternative prompts for the same need, and try the best one. And step six. Save your best prompt in your library, with a note on what worked.

Let's recap. Image prompts describe a picture: subject, action, setting, style, composition, lighting, mood and format. Specific visual details beat generic adjectives like "beautiful" or "professional". Keep in-image text minimal, and add headlines yourself in a design tool. Iterate one change at a time, and save the prompts that work. And use images responsibly. That means no misleading depictions of real people or products, and always check usage rights.
