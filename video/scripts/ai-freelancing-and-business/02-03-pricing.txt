Welcome to Make Money with AI, module 2, lesson 3: Pricing Your Services in Rupees and Dollars.

Pricing is the topic beginners worry about most. Charge too little and you burn out. Charge too much without proof and nobody replies. This lesson gives you a practical method to set prices in rupees and dollars that you can defend, adjust and grow.

One important note before we start. Every number in this lesson is an illustrative example to show the method. Rates vary hugely by city, client, skill, niche and platform. Nothing here is a promise or a guaranteed market rate.

There are three ways to price.

The first is hourly pricing. You charge for time. It's simple, but it has a problem in the AI era. If AI helps you finish faster, you earn less. Hourly works best for open-ended tasks like ongoing support or consulting calls.

The second is project pricing. You charge a fixed amount for a defined deliverable, like "ten product descriptions" or "clean and deduplicate this customer list". Clients like knowing the cost upfront, and you benefit from your own efficiency. The risk is underestimating effort, so define scope carefully.

The third is retainer pricing. That's a fixed monthly fee for an agreed set of ongoing work. For example, twelve social posts and a monthly report. Retainers give you predictable income, and the client predictable support.

Most freelancers starting out use project pricing for one-time work and retainers for recurring work.

Now let's calculate your floor price. Before looking at the market, work out the minimum you need. This is your floor, the price below which a project isn't worth taking.

Here's an example. Say your monthly target income is thirty thousand rupees, and your monthly costs, like internet and tools, are two thousand rupees. That's thirty-two thousand rupees needed in total. If you have sixty billable hours available per month, divide thirty-two thousand by sixty. Your floor hourly rate is about five hundred and thirty-three rupees per hour. The calculation is in the lesson notes below this video.

Remember that not every working hour is billable. Outreach, learning, admin and invoicing take time too. Many freelancers find only half to two-thirds of their working hours are billable.

To convert this to a project price, estimate the hours, multiply by your rate, and then add a buffer of twenty to thirty percent for revisions and communication.

Next, check the market. Look at what others offer for similar work. Browse Fiverr and Upwork listings for your service and note the range. Look at LinkedIn posts from freelancers in your niche who mention their packages. And ask peers in freelancer communities.

You'll see a wide spread. That's normal. Price reflects niche, proof and communication, not just the task.

The lesson notes include a table of example price ranges, illustrative only, that a beginner might consider. Treat them as a starting point for your own research, not as market data. Let me give you a few examples. A researched and edited thousand-word blog post might range from fifteen hundred to five thousand rupees for Indian clients, or forty to a hundred and fifty dollars for international clients. A social media month with twelve posts and captions might be six thousand to twenty thousand rupees, or two hundred to six hundred dollars. And transcription plus subtitles for a thirty-minute video might be one thousand to four thousand rupees, or thirty to a hundred and twenty dollars. The table also covers WhatsApp Business setup, data cleaning, presentations and simple automation.

Your actual pricing will depend on your skills, proof and the client's budget. Some clients will pay more, and many beginners start lower to win early projects. Platform fees and payment charges also reduce what you receive, so check current fees before you quote.

Now, pricing for Indian versus international clients. You don't need one price for everyone. Many freelancers keep separate rate cards. Indian small businesses often have tight budgets and prefer clear, all-inclusive packages, and they frequently pay via U P I or bank transfer. International clients may have larger budgets, but there's also more competition, time-zone challenges, and higher expectations on communication. Payment fees and currency conversion apply too.

Quote international clients in their currency, usually U S dollars, to avoid confusion.

Next, present your prices as packages. Offering three tiers helps clients choose, rather than just decide yes or no. Here's an example for social media. Basic is eight posts per month, with captions and designs. Standard is twelve posts per month, with captions, designs and a content calendar. And Premium is twelve posts, plus four reels scripts, plus a monthly performance summary.

Make the middle option the one you most want to sell.

What about when a client asks, "Can you do it cheaper?" Here's the thing. Instead of dropping your price, adjust the scope. You might say something like this: "I understand budgets are tight. For six thousand rupees I can do eight posts instead of twelve, with the same quality and review process. Would that work for you?"

This protects your rate while still helping the client.

So when should you raise prices? There are a few signals. You're consistently busy, clients accept quotes without hesitation, or you now have testimonials and results to show. Raise prices for new clients first, and give existing clients notice before changing theirs.

You can also use AI to help with pricing. The prompt in the lesson notes gives the AI your service, niche, floor rate and package contents. It asks it to estimate hours for each step, add a twenty-five percent buffer, suggest a project price, and then suggest Basic, Standard and Premium tiers, with the calculation shown in a table. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

Treat the output as a calculator, not a market authority. You know your speed and your clients better.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about fifteen minutes, so pause the video and work through it.

Step one. Calculate your floor hourly rate using your own numbers.

Step two. List every step in your core offer from Lesson 3, and estimate the hours for each.

Step three. Calculate a project price with a twenty-five percent buffer.

Step four. Browse at least five similar listings on a freelance platform and note the range.

Step five. Create three tiers, and fill in the price section of your one-pager. Add a note that says, "Prices reviewed every three months."

Let's recap. Use project pricing for one-time work and retainers for recurring work, while hourly suits open-ended tasks. Calculate a floor price from your real costs and available billable hours. Market ranges vary widely, and all the figures here are illustrative examples, not promises. And offer tiered packages, adjusting scope rather than cutting price.
