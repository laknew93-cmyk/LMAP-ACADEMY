Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 2, lesson 2: SUMIFS, COUNTIFS and Conditional Totals.

Most MIS questions are some version of "how much, how many, where and when". SUMIFS, COUNTIFS and AVERAGEIFS answer them in a single cell. In this lesson, you'll learn the patterns you'll use every month, and how to get AI to write them correctly.

Let's start with the shape of SUMIFS. The sum range comes first. Then you add pairs: where to look, and what to match. All the ranges must be the same size. Now, the older SUMIF puts the sum range last, and that's a frequent source of confusion. So prefer SUMIFS, even for a single condition.

Let's use Pink City Fabrics' sales, stored as an Excel Table named Sales. For total sales from the Online channel, it's equals SUMIFS, then Sales Amount, then Sales Store, then "Online". For total UPI sales from the Vaishali store, you add a second pair. Sales Store equals "Jaipur Vaishali", and Sales Payment Mode equals "UPI". Simple.

Next, take your criteria from cells, not typed text. Hard-coding "Online" means editing the formula every time. Put the criteria in cells and reference them instead, so one formula fills a whole summary grid. Imagine a summary sheet with store names down column A, and payment modes across row 1. In cell B2, you write a SUMIFS that matches the store against A2 and the payment mode against B1. The trick is the dollar signs. You lock the column for the store, so it's dollar A 2, and you lock the row for the payment mode, so it's B dollar 1. Now you can drag the formula across and down. The exact formula is in the lesson notes below the video. If the AI gives you a formula without dollar signs and you need to fill a grid, just ask: "Add the right absolute references so I can drag this across B2 to E4."

Now let's look at comparison operators and dates. Criteria can include operators in quotes, joined to values with an ampersand. For example, you can sum amounts where the amount is greater than or equal to five thousand.

Date ranges are the most common need. For October 2026 sales, you sum the amount where the date is on or after the first of October 2026, and before the first of November 2026, using the DATE function for each. Even better, make it driven by a cell. Say H1 holds any date in the month you want. Then EOMONTH of H1, minus one, plus one, gives the first day of that month. And EOMONTH of H1, zero, gives the last day. Use those two as your start and end. Change H1, and the whole report updates. The full formulas are in the lesson notes. One caution: this only works when the date column holds real dates. Lesson 2.3 and Module 3 show you how to fix text dates.

What about Indian financial year totals? For F Y 2026 27, that's the first of April 2026 to the thirty-first of March 2027. You use the same pattern with those two dates. And always tell the AI "Indian financial year, April to March". Otherwise, it may assume a calendar year.

Next, wildcards for partial matches. There are five patterns worth knowing. "Jaipur" followed by an asterisk means "starts with Jaipur". An asterisk, then "Saree", then another asterisk, means "contains Saree anywhere". The not-equal sign followed by "Online" means "everything except Online". The not-equal sign on its own means non-blank cells. And two empty quotes mean blank cells, which is useful for things like "leads with no owner assigned". For example, to count LeadLoop leads with no owner, it's COUNTIFS on the owner column, matching an empty string.

COUNTIFS and AVERAGEIFS follow the same pattern. COUNTIFS has no sum range. It just counts rows meeting all the conditions. So, won deals from LinkedIn is COUNTIFS where source is "LinkedIn" and stage is "Won".

Now let's look at an example where things go wrong. You want a win rate for LinkedIn, which is won deals divided by all closed deals. Suppose an assistant hands you a formula where the top and the bottom are both "count of LinkedIn deals that are Won". Spot the problem? The numerator and denominator are identical, so the answer is always one hundred percent. A copy-paste slip like this is easy to miss in a long formula. The correct denominator counts Won plus Lost. The corrected formula is in the lesson notes. Here's the lesson: this is exactly the kind of error AI tools occasionally make in longer formulas. Read the explanation, and sanity-check the result. A one hundred percent win rate should make you suspicious.

For the average deal value of won LinkedIn deals, use AVERAGEIFS on the deal value column, with the same two conditions. If there are no matching rows, AVERAGEIFS returns a divide by zero error. You can wrap it in IFERROR and return zero. Or better, return a dash, so nobody mistakes "no data" for "zero".

What about OR conditions? SUMIFS combines conditions with AND. So for sales that are UPI or Card, you add two SUMIFS together. One for UPI, plus one for Card. In Excel 365, you can also put both values in curly braces as an array constant, and wrap the SUMIFS in SUM. Both versions are in the lesson notes.

Finally, here's how to get AI to build a whole summary grid. The prompt in the lesson notes describes the Sales table and its columns, says categories are down column A of a Summary sheet, store names are across row 1, and H1 holds a date in the reporting month. Then it asks for one formula in B2 that can be dragged to D5, giving the total amount for that category and store in H1's month, and asks the AI to explain the dollar signs. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about twenty minutes, so pause the video and work through it. Step one. Ask an AI to generate fifty rows of fake sales data for Pink City Fabrics across September and October 2026, with columns Date, Store, Category, Payment Mode and Amount. Paste it in, and convert it to a Table named Sales. Step two. Build a Summary sheet with categories down and stores across. Write one SUMIFS in B2 that you drag across the grid, driven by a month cell in H1. Step three. Change H1 from October to September, and check that the grid updates. Step four. Verify one cell manually. Filter the Sales table for that category, store and month, and compare the Sum shown in the status bar with your formula. And step five. Add a row with COUNTIFS for the number of invoices, and a row with the average invoice value.

Let's recap. SUMIFS puts the sum range first, so use it even for one condition. Reference criteria cells with the right dollar-sign locking, so one formula fills a whole grid. For month or financial year totals, use a greater-than-or-equal start date and a less-than-or-equal end date, built with DATE or EOMONTH. SUMIFS is AND only, so add separate SUMIFS for OR conditions. And sanity-check your ratios. A suspicious one hundred percent often means a wrong denominator.
