Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 1, lesson 2: Describing Your Sheet So AI Gets Formulas Right.

The single biggest reason people get wrong formulas from AI is simple. The AI can't see their sheet. In this lesson, you'll learn an easy way to describe your data, so the first answer is usually the right one.

Let's start with why vague questions get vague formulas. Compare two requests. The first one says: "Give me a formula to calculate total sales for Jaipur." That's it. The second one is much more specific. It says you're using Excel, Microsoft 365. The sheet is called Sales, with headers in row 1 and data from A2 to K1050. Column C is the store, with values like "Jaipur MI Road", "Jaipur Vaishali" and "Online". Column J is the amount including G S T. Column B is the date, stored as real Excel dates. And the goal is the total of column J for both Jaipur stores combined, for October 2026 only, with the formula going on a summary sheet and a brief explanation. Both prompts are in the lesson notes below this video.

Here's the thing. The first request gets you a generic SUMIF that refers to imaginary columns, and it probably misses that "Jaipur" appears inside two different store names. The second gets you something you can paste directly. It's a SUMIFS that adds up the amount column, where the store starts with "Jaipur", and the date is on or after the first of October and before the first of November. The full formula is in the lesson notes. Notice two things. There's a wildcard, Jaipur followed by an asterisk symbol, which matches any store name starting with Jaipur. And the date range is written as "on or after the first of October, and before the first of November." That's a pattern you'll reuse constantly.

Now let me give you a checklist for every formula request. It's called the CONTEXT template, and each letter stands for something. C is for cell layout. That's your sheet name, header row, data range and column letters. O is for output. Where does the formula go, and what should it return? A number, some text, or a list? N is for the nature of values. Are they numbers, text, real dates or text that looks like dates? Are there blanks? T is for tool and version. Excel 2016, Microsoft 365, or Google Sheets? E is for examples. Give two or three sample rows and the answer you expect for them. X is for exceptions. What should happen with blanks, errors or missing matches? And the final T is for teach. Ask for an explanation, so you understand what you paste. You don't need every item every time, but the more you include, the better the result.

There's a reusable prompt built from this template in the lesson notes. It has slots for your tool, your sheet and data range, each relevant column with its type and example values, your goal in plain words, where the formula goes, a few sample rows with the expected result, your edge cases, and finally a request for the formula plus an explanation of each part in simple English. Copy it and keep it handy.

Next, paste sample rows, not the whole sheet. The fastest way to give context is to copy a few rows straight from your sheet into the chat. Spreadsheets paste as tab-separated text, which assistants read well. Five to ten rows, including the header, is plenty. There are two rules here. First, mask anything personal before you paste. Customer names, phone numbers, PAN, Aadhaar, bank details. Replace them with dummy values. Lesson 1.3 covers this properly. Second, include an awkward row. If some dates are text, or some amounts are blank, include one of those rows so the AI handles it.

Now, let's talk about Excel Tables and named columns. If your data is formatted as an Excel Table, formulas become much easier to read, and much easier to ask about. To do this, select the range, press Control T, and give the table a name, like Sales, under Table Design. Then your formula reads naturally. For example, equals SUMIFS, then Sales Amount, then Sales Store, then "Jaipur" with a wildcard. So just tell the AI, "My data is an Excel Table named Sales with columns Invoice No, Date, Store and Amount." It'll write structured references like that, and they keep working when new rows are added. Google Sheets has a similar feature under Format, then Convert to table. And named ranges work in both tools.

Here's another habit. Always ask for the formula and the reasoning. End your request with "explain each part." This does two things. It helps you learn, so next month you can write a similar formula yourself. And it exposes mistakes. If the explanation says "this looks up the value in column D", but your customer I Ds are in column B, you've caught the error before it reaches your manager.

Next, iterate, don't restart. If the formula gives an N A error, a VALUE error, or just a wrong number, don't start a new chat. Reply in the same conversation and say what happened. Here's the prompt: "I pasted it in F2. It returns N A for row 7, where the Lead ID is L L dash zero zero four two, with a trailing space. The other rows work. How do I fix this?" Now the AI knows the exact problem, and it'll likely suggest wrapping the lookup value in TRIM. Giving the error message, the cell and the specific value is the debugging equivalent of the CONTEXT template.

You can also ask for alternatives, and pick the one that suits your team. Here's the prompt: "Give me two versions: one using XLOOKUP for Excel 365, and one using INDEX MATCH that works in Excel 2016. Which is easier for a colleague to maintain?" This really matters in Indian offices, where the same file gets opened on a new laptop at head office and an old desktop at the branch.

Now, try it yourself. Take about fifteen minutes, and use LeadLoop's leads sheet, or any sheet of yours, masked. Pause the video and follow along. Step one. Create a small sheet with these headers in row 1: Lead ID, Created On, Source, Owner, City, Stage and Deal Value. Add eight rows of made-up data, with sources LinkedIn, Website, Referral and IndiaMART, and stages New, Demo, Won and Lost. Step two. Without the template, ask an AI: "Formula to get total won deal value from LinkedIn." Paste the result and see if it works. Step three. Now use the CONTEXT template. Include your columns, your tool, three sample rows, and the expected answer you calculated by hand. Step four. Compare the two answers. Did the second one work first time? Did the explanation match your columns? And step five. Save your filled template in a notes file. You'll reuse it all through the course.

Let's recap. The AI can't see your sheet, so describe the layout, data types, tool version and expected output. Paste five to ten masked sample rows, including one awkward row. Use Excel Tables, with Control T, so formulas use readable names like Sales Amount. Always ask for an explanation. It teaches you, and it catches errors. And when something breaks, reply with the exact error, cell and value, instead of starting over.
