Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 1, lesson 1: Becoming the Data Person with AI.

In almost every Indian office, there's one person everyone goes to when the numbers don't add up. This course will make you that person. You'll use AI assistants to do in minutes what used to take a full evening of fighting with spreadsheets.

So, what does the data person actually do? Forget job titles for a moment. Whether it's a Jaipur garment retailer, a Pune logistics firm or a Chennai CA practice, the data person is simply the one who can do four things reliably. First, get the data into shape. That means merging the branch-wise sheets, fixing the dates and removing the duplicate invoices. Second, summarise it. For example, "What were our sales by category last month, and how does that compare with September?" Third, explain it. That means turning a pivot table into three sentences a busy director understands. And fourth, repeat it. You produce the same report every month without rebuilding it from scratch.

Here's the good news. None of this needs a statistics degree. It needs comfort with formulas, pivot tables, a charting tool, and the habit of checking your work. AI assistants now make the first three much faster, and they help you design the fourth.

Now let's be honest about what AI changes, and what it doesn't. Think about writing a tricky formula. Without AI, you Google it, copy something from a forum, adapt it, and break it. With AI, you describe what you want in plain English and get a formula plus an explanation. Or say a colleague left you a three-line nested IF. Without AI, you'd stare at it for twenty minutes. With AI, you paste it and ask, "explain this step by step." Cleaning inconsistent city names used to mean manual find and replace. Now you can ask for a mapping table or a cleaning formula. A first-pass analysis of a C S V file used to mean building pivots one by one. Now you can upload the file to a chat assistant and ask questions. And the summary email? Instead of staring at a blank page, you ask for a draft and then fix it with your own judgement. The full comparison is in a table in the lesson notes below this video.

But here's what AI does not change. You're still responsible for the numbers. AI assistants can misread a column, pick the wrong date format, or confidently state a total that's off by a few lakh. So throughout this course, you'll learn one simple discipline: AI drafts, you verify. Lesson 4.2 is entirely about verification, and every project includes a checking step.

Let's talk about the tools we'll use. You don't need all of them, so pick what your workplace already uses. First, a chat assistant such as ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini. The free versions are enough for formula help. File upload and data analysis features vary by plan and change often, so check what your account offers. Second, Microsoft Excel or Google Sheets. Ideally that's Microsoft 365, which has XLOOKUP, dynamic arrays, and Copilot features for eligible plans. Google Sheets is free with any Google account. Third, Power BI Desktop. It's free to download on Windows, and we use it in Module 5. If you're on a Mac, you can follow along with Google Sheets or Looker Studio for the dashboard project. And fourth, Google Colab. That one's optional, for the light Python lesson. It runs in your browser, so there's nothing to install.

Next, meet the datasets. Instead of abstract examples, we'll keep coming back to a few realistic Indian datasets, so you can see how the techniques build on each other.

The first is Pink City Fabrics in Jaipur. It's a retailer with two stores and an online channel. Their monthly sales sheet has columns like invoice number, date, store, customer city, category, item, quantity, rate, amount including G S T, G S T rate, and payment mode. Amounts include G S T at five percent or twelve percent depending on the item. Payment modes are UPI, card, cash and cash on delivery. And like every real sheet, it's messy. It has duplicate invoices, cities spelt three different ways, like "Jaipur", "jaipur" with a trailing space, and "J P R", plus dates that are sometimes stored as text.

The second is LeadLoop, a Bengaluru SaaS startup. They have a leads sheet exported from their CRM, with columns for lead I D, created on, source, owner, company, city, stage, deal value in rupees, and closed on. Sources include LinkedIn, Website, Referral and IndiaMART. Stages run from New all the way to Won or Lost. The exact column lists are in the lesson notes. Later on, we'll also add a small expense register for a Chennai CA firm, and a branch MIS for a Nagpur distributor.

So how does each lesson work? Every lesson follows the same pattern. There's a concept, worked examples with real formulas or prompts, and a short "Try it yourself" exercise. Please do the exercises. Reading about pivot tables builds no skill. Building one does.

The four projects are where it all comes together. First, you'll clean and analyse a one-thousand-row sales dataset, in Module 3. Second, you'll build a pivot-based monthly MIS report that refreshes with new data, also in Module 3. Third, you'll build a one-page dashboard in Power BI or Google Sheets, in Module 5. And fourth, in the final lesson, you'll write a five-insight executive summary from data. Together, these make a solid portfolio if you're applying for MIS, analyst or finance roles on Naukri or LinkedIn. You can describe exactly what you built and show a sanitised version.

A quick word on Excel versions. Indian offices run everything from Excel 2013 to the latest Microsoft 365. Some functions in this course, like XLOOKUP, FILTER, UNIQUE and TEXTBEFORE, only exist in newer versions. Whenever we use one, we'll give you an older-version alternative. And here's a good habit: always tell the AI which version you use. A formula that works in Microsoft 365 can show a NAME error in Excel 2016.

Now it's your turn. Try it yourself. Spend about fifteen minutes setting up and taking stock. Pause the video and work through these steps. Step one. Open the spreadsheet tool you use at work. In Excel, go to File, then Account, and note your version. In Google Sheets, just note that you're on Sheets. Step two. Open your chat assistant and ask: "In my version of Excel or Sheets, is XLOOKUP available? If not, what is the closest alternative?" Then check the answer by typing equals XLOOKUP and an opening bracket in a cell, and see if it autocompletes. Step three. Pick one real, or realistic, sheet you deal with regularly. Write down its columns, roughly how many rows it has, and the one question your manager asks about it most often. Step four. Write down the most painful step in producing that report today. That's the problem you'll solve by the end of this course.

Let's recap. The data person cleans, summarises, explains and repeats, and AI speeds up all four. AI drafts, you verify. You remain accountable for every number you share. Always tell the AI your tool and version, because formulas differ between Excel 2016, Microsoft 365 and Google Sheets. And we'll work with realistic Indian datasets, a Jaipur retailer and a Bengaluru startup, throughout the course.
