Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 5, lesson 2: Project: Build a One-Page Dashboard.

A good dashboard answers a manager's top questions in under a minute, on one screen, without explanation. In this project, you'll choose the right charts, design a clean layout, and build a one-page dashboard in Power BI or Google Sheets.

Start with the audience, not the charts. Before opening any tool, write down four things. Who looks at it? Here, that's Mr. Agarwal, the owner of Pink City Fabrics. When? Monday mornings, on his phone or laptop, for two or three minutes. What decisions does it support? Which store needs attention, which categories to restock before Diwali, and whether online cash on delivery is getting risky. And which three to five questions must it answer at a glance?

You can use AI to sharpen this. The prompt in the lesson notes describes the business, a Jaipur clothing retailer with two stores and an online channel, and lists the data columns. It explains that the owner reviews the dashboard weekly to decide on stock and staff. Then it asks for the five most useful questions the dashboard should answer, the KPI for each, and the best chart type, all kept to one page. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. Pick what makes sense, and drop anything decorative.

Now, choosing the right chart. It depends on the type of question. For "how much in total?", use a KPI card with a comparison, like versus last month. Avoid gauges with no context. For "how has it changed over time?", use a line chart, or a column chart if there are only a few periods. Avoid pie charts here. For "how do categories compare?", use a horizontal bar chart, sorted. Avoid 3D bars and unsorted bars. For "what's the share or mix?", use a one hundred percent stacked bar, or a pie or donut with only two to four slices. Avoid pies with eight slices. For "how do two measures relate?", use a scatter plot, and avoid dual-axis charts unless they're very clear. And for detail lookups, use a small table or matrix with conditional formatting, but avoid huge tables on the main page. The full table is in the lesson notes.

There are three rules that improve most dashboards immediately. One, sort bars by value, largest first. Two, label directly. Show values on the bars, instead of making people read axes. And three, use colour for meaning, not decoration. Use one neutral colour for everything, and one highlight colour for what needs attention.

Next, the layout. Use a simple Z pattern, because people read the top-left first. There's a layout sketch in the lesson notes. At the very top, there's a title, "Pink City Fabrics, Sales Dashboard", with a month slicer. Below that, a row of four KPI cards: total sales, month-on-month growth percent, invoices, and average invoice value. In the middle, two charts side by side. On the left, a sales trend by month, as a line chart with one line per channel. On the right, sales by category, as a sorted bar chart with month-on-month percent. Below those, the payment mode mix by channel, as a one hundred percent stacked bar, next to a table of the top ten items. And at the bottom, a notes box with two or three lines of commentary, and the "data as of" date. So, KPIs across the top, trend and breakdown in the middle, and detail at the bottom.

Option A: build it in Power BI, using the model and measures from Lesson 5.1. There are seven steps. One, KPI cards. Insert a Card for each of Total Sales, Month-on-Month Growth, Invoices and Average Invoice Value. Format currency with the rupee sign and no decimals. Two, slicers. Add a Month Name slicer, in dropdown style, and a Store slicer. Three, the trend. Add a line chart, with Month Start from the Date table on the X axis, Total Sales as values, and Store in the legend. Four, category. Add a clustered bar chart, with Category on the Y axis and Total Sales as values. Sort it descending, and turn on data labels. Five, payment mix. Add a one hundred percent stacked bar chart, with Store on the axis and Payment Mode as the legend. Six, top items. Add a table visual with Item and Total Sales. Then in the visual's filter pane, set Item's filter type to Top N, showing the top ten by Total Sales. And seven, conditional formatting. In the category chart, colour the bars by month-on-month growth, with red for negative, using the f x rules.

Then check the interactions. Clicking a bar in one visual filters the others. If that's confusing for a particular visual, use Format, then Edit interactions, to turn it off.

There's also a useful extra measure for the title. It uses SELECTEDVALUE to build a label that says "Sales for", followed by the selected month, or "multiple months" if more than one is selected. The code is in the lesson notes. Put it in a Card at the top, so the dashboard always states what period it shows.

Option B: build it in Google Sheets. There are six steps. One, put your clean data on a Data sheet. Two, create a Calc sheet with summary tables, using QUERY or SUMIFS, driven by a month cell. For example, there's a QUERY formula in the lesson notes that gives sales by category for the month in cell B1, sorted largest first. The date has to be inserted into the query as text, in year-month-day form, which is why the formula uses TEXT. Ask an AI to adapt it to your column letters, and test it against a SUMIFS total. Three, on a Dashboard sheet, add KPI cells with large fonts that reference the Calc sheet, and show month-on-month percent with conditional formatting. Four, insert charts from the Calc tables, under Insert, then Chart. Use a line for the trend, a bar for category, and a stacked bar for payment mix. Five, add a data validation dropdown for the month, under Data, then Data validation, using a list of month dates, so users can switch periods. And six, hide gridlines, under View, Show, Gridlines, for a clean look. Looker Studio, which is free from Google, is another option if you want a more dashboard-like, interactive feel on top of Sheets data.

Now, review it with AI, and with a human. Take a screenshot of your finished dashboard, using masked or practice data, and give it to a chat assistant that accepts images. The prompt in the lesson notes asks it to critique the dashboard as a data visualisation expert. Is the most important information prominent? Is any chart the wrong type? Is there clutter to remove? Is anything ambiguous, like units, periods, or whether amounts include G S T? And it asks for the top five fixes, in priority order. Then show it to one real person for sixty seconds, and ask: "What's the main thing this tells you?" If they can't answer, simplify.

Before you finish, run through the final checklist in the lesson notes. The title states the business, the period, and a "data as of" date. Units are clear: rupees, including or excluding G S T, lakhs or rupees. KPIs have comparisons, versus last month or target, not just raw numbers. Bars are sorted, values are labelled, there's no 3D, and you use two or three colours at most. Slicers work, and don't break any visual. Totals match your Excel pivot or control total. And it fits on one screen without scrolling.

Now, try it yourself. Allow sixty to ninety minutes for this project, so pause the video and get started. Step one. Write down the audience, the decisions, and three to five key questions for your dashboard. Step two. Sketch the layout on paper, using the template from this lesson. Step three. Build it in Power BI, that's Option A, or in Google Sheets, that's Option B, using your clean Pink City Fabrics data. Step four. Verify every KPI against your Excel results. And step five. Get an AI critique from a screenshot, and make at least three improvements.

Let's recap. Start from the audience's decisions and three to five questions, not from the charts available. Match the chart to the question: lines for trends, sorted bars for comparisons, and cards with comparisons for KPIs. Use a layout with KPIs at the top, trends in the middle and detail at the bottom, that fits on one screen. Make periods, units and G S T treatment explicit on the page. And verify your totals, and get both an AI critique and a quick human test.
