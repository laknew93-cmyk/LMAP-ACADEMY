Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 3, lesson 1: Cleaning Messy Data: Duplicates, Names and Dates.

Every analysis is only as good as the data underneath it. In this lesson, you'll get a repeatable cleaning routine for the mess Indian business data usually comes with. Duplicate invoices, five spellings of the same city, and dates that are secretly text.

Let's start with rule zero: never clean the original. Make a copy of the raw file, and keep it untouched. Do all your cleaning on the copy, ideally in a new sheet called Clean. If something goes wrong, you can always go back. Many analysts also keep a short Cleaning Log sheet listing what they changed. That's really useful when your manager asks why your total differs from the billing software's.

Step one: profile before you fix. Before changing anything, understand what's there. For each important column, ask a few questions. How many rows are there, and are any completely blank? How many unique values are there? Five stores is plausible. Twenty-three "stores" means spelling chaos. Are numbers really numbers, and are dates really dates? And are there any obvious outliers, like a zero-rupee invoice, or a typo of ninety-nine lakh, ninety-nine thousand, nine hundred and ninety-nine rupees?

There are some quick profiling formulas for this. COUNTA counts non-blank entries. COUNTBLANK counts blanks. ROWS of UNIQUE gives you the number of distinct values, in Excel 365 or Sheets. A SUMPRODUCT of ISTEXT on the date column counts dates stored as text. And MIN and MAX on the amount column show you the range. They're all in the lesson notes below the video. Here's a faster trick: just click the filter dropdown on a column. The list of values shows spelling variants instantly.

You can also paste a column's distinct values into an AI and ask for a profile. Here's the prompt: "Here are the distinct values in the Customer City column of a Jaipur retailer's sales data. Group the ones that are probably the same city and suggest one standard spelling for each. Flag anything you're unsure about rather than guessing." Then paste your list.

Step two: remove duplicates, carefully. Duplicates appear when a billing export is run twice, or two branch files overlap. But not every repeated row is a duplicate. Two customers can buy the same saree on the same day for the same price. So first, decide what makes a row unique. For Pink City Fabrics, it's the invoice number plus the item code, because one invoice can have several items.

Flag duplicates before deleting anything. Add a helper column with a COUNTIFS that uses an expanding range. The range starts locked at row 2 and ends at the current row, for both the invoice number and the item code. That counts how many times this combination has appeared so far. So the first occurrence shows one, and repeats show two, three and so on. The formula is in the lesson notes. Filter for values greater than one, review them, and then delete. Alternatively, use Data, then Remove Duplicates, and tick only the key columns. Note the count Excel reports in your Cleaning Log, something like "Removed 37 duplicate invoice lines".

Step three: standardise text. There are three typical fixes, in order. First, remove extra spaces with TRIM. Text pasted from websites can contain non-breaking spaces that TRIM misses. For those, substitute character 160 with a normal space first, then TRIM. Second, fix the case. PROPER of TRIM turns " jaipur" with a leading space into a clean "Jaipur". And third, map variants to a standard name. Build a small two-column mapping table, with Raw on the left and Standard on the right. For example, J P R maps to Jaipur. Jaipur City maps to Jaipur. Bangalore and Bengaluru Urban both map to Bengaluru. And Gurgaon maps to Gurugram. That table is in the lesson notes.

Then you use XLOOKUP. You look up the cleaned value, that's PROPER of TRIM, in the Raw column of your city map, and return the Standard name. And if it's not found, you just return the cleaned value itself. The formula is in the lesson notes. One catch: the Raw column must contain the PROPER-cased versions, so "Jpr" rather than all-caps "JPR", for this to match. Or apply PROPER to the map too. An AI is great at drafting this mapping table from your list of distinct values. But review every row. It might merge "Raipur" into "Jaipur" if they look like a typo.

You can also use Find and Replace, with Control H, for quick one-off fixes. And Flash Fill, with Control E in Excel, extracts patterns. Type the result you want in the first two cells, press Control E, and Excel fills in the rest.

Step four: fix dates. Date problems are the most dangerous, because they often look fine. To spot text dates, remember that real dates align right by default, and text aligns left. ISNUMBER of the cell returns FALSE for a text date.

Then there's the DD MM versus MM DD confusion. Indian files use day, month, year. But a system set to US format may read "05 slash 10 slash 2026" as the tenth of May, instead of the fifth of October. Here are the symptoms. No dates after the twelfth of any month appear in some months. Or "13 slash 10 slash 2026" stays as text, while "12 slash 10 slash 2026" converts.

There are three fixes. First, Text to Columns. Select the column, then go to Data, Text to Columns, choose Delimited, click Next twice, choose Date, D M Y, and click Finish. That converts day-month-year text into real dates correctly. Second, for fixed-format text like "05 dash 10 dash 2026", use the DATE function, taking the year from the right four characters, the month from the middle two, and the day from the left two. And third, for mixed formats, where a column has some real dates and some text, wrap that in an IF. If it's already a number, keep it. Otherwise, convert it. The formulas are in the lesson notes below the video. After converting, format the column as a date and check that all values are within the expected period. MIN and MAX on the column catch strays like 1900 or 2062.

Step five: fix numbers. To remove currency text, use VALUE with SUBSTITUTE to strip out the rupee symbol and the commas. Numbers stored as text show a small green triangle. Select them, click the warning icon, and choose Convert to Number. And decide what blanks mean. A blank discount probably means zero. A blank amount means something is wrong. Don't fill blanks blindly.

Step six: handle outliers thoughtfully. Sort by amount, largest first, and look at the top ten and bottom ten. A four and a half lakh rupee invoice, from a retailer whose average bill is twenty-five hundred rupees, could be a wholesale order or a typo. Ask, don't delete. Flag it in a column, and note it in your Cleaning Log.

Finally, Power Query: cleaning you only do once. If you receive the same messy export every month, use Power Query. You'll find it under Data, then Get Data, in Excel 2016 and later on Windows. Every cleaning step, like trim, replace, change type, or remove duplicates, gets recorded. Next month, you drop in the new file and click Refresh. You'll use this in the automated report lesson. And AI assistants can also explain or write Power Query steps, in its M language, if you describe what you need.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about twenty minutes, so pause the video and follow along. Step one. Ask an AI to generate forty rows of messy sales data. Include city spelling variants, like Jaipur, J P R and lowercase jaipur, three duplicate invoice lines, dates mixing day-month-year text and real dates, and amounts like "Rs 1,250". Step two. Profile it. Count the distinct cities, the text dates, and the minimum and maximum amount. Step three. Flag and remove duplicates using the COUNTIFS helper column. Step four. Build a city mapping table and apply it with XLOOKUP. Step five. Convert all dates to real dates, and amounts to numbers. And step six. Write a five-line Cleaning Log of what you changed.

Let's recap. Never clean the original. Work on a copy, and keep a Cleaning Log. Profile first: distinct values, text dates, blanks, min and max. Define what makes a row unique before removing duplicates. Use a mapping table for name variants. Let AI draft it, but review every row. And check dates for day-month versus month-day problems. Text to Columns with D M Y is your friend.
