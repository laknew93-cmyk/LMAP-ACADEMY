Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 5, lesson 1: Power BI Basics and DAX Measures with AI.

Power BI is where many Excel users go next. It handles bigger data, connects several tables cleanly, and produces interactive dashboards that managers can explore themselves. In this lesson, you'll go from zero to your first model and your first DAX measures, with AI explaining every formula.

Let's describe Power BI in one paragraph. Power BI Desktop is a free Windows application from Microsoft. You load data, from Excel, C S V files, folders or databases. You clean it with the same Power Query you met in Lesson 3.4. You model it by connecting tables. You write measures in a formula language called DAX. And you visualise it on report pages. To share reports online with colleagues, organisations use the Power BI service, which generally needs a paid licence, so check with your IT team. But for learning, Desktop is all you need. If you're on a Mac, you can follow the concepts here, and build the dashboard project in Google Sheets or Looker Studio instead.

Step one: load the data. Open Power BI Desktop, click Get data, then Excel workbook, or Text slash C S V, and select your clean Pink City Fabrics file from the Lesson 3.3 project. Tick the Sales table, and click Transform Data to open Power Query. Check three things. Date should be a Date type, with the calendar icon, not text. Amount and quantity should be numbers. And text columns should be trimmed. Then click Close and Apply. Also load a small Products table, with item, category and G S T rate, and, if you have one, a Targets table, with store, month and target.

Step two: build a date table. Power BI's time calculations work best with a dedicated date table. On the Modeling tab, click New table, and enter the DAX from the lesson notes. Here's what it does. It creates a calendar of every date from the first of April 2026 to the thirty-first of March 2027. And it adds four columns: the month start date, a month name like "Oct dash 26", the financial year label, and the financial year quarter. The code is in the lesson notes below the video. This covers the Indian financial year 2026 27, so extend the range if your data spans more years. Then select the table, use Mark as date table, and choose the Date column. Here's a tip. To make Month Name sort as April, May, June and so on, instead of alphabetically, select it and set Sort by column to Month Start.

Step three: create relationships. Switch to Model view, which is the diagram icon on the left. Drag the Date column of your Date table onto the Date column of Sales. And drag Item in Products onto Item in Sales. You'll see lines with a "one" on the lookup side, and an asterisk, meaning "many", on the Sales side. This is called a star schema. There's a central fact table, Sales, surrounded by lookup tables, also called dimension tables. It replaces all those XLOOKUP columns you'd add in Excel.

If you ask an AI for help, describe your tables. The prompt in the lesson notes lists all four tables with their columns, then asks how to set up the relationships, and whether it's a problem that Targets is monthly while Sales is daily. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. The short answer you'll likely get is that Targets is at a different grain, so you relate it through Month Start, or through a separate Store table. That's a good question to explore with AI once the basics feel comfortable.

Step four: measures versus calculated columns. This is the concept that confuses every Excel user, so let's get it clear. A calculated column is computed once per row, and stored in the table. In Excel terms, it's like a helper column. An example would be a Taxable column, equal to the amount divided by one plus the related product's G S T rate. You use calculated columns for row-level attributes, and for slicing and grouping. A measure, on the other hand, is computed on the fly, for whatever is filtered in the visual. In Excel terms, it's like a pivot table value. An example is Total Sales, which is just the SUM of the amount. You use measures for totals, ratios and comparisons. There's a comparison table in the lesson notes. And here's the rule of thumb: if you'll add it up or compare it, make it a measure.

Step five: your first DAX measures. Select the Sales table, click New measure, and add four measures, one at a time. Total Sales is the SUM of amount. Total Quantity is the SUM of quantity. Invoices is a DISTINCTCOUNT of invoice number. And Average Invoice Value uses DIVIDE, with total sales on top and invoices below. The code is in the lesson notes below the video. Why DIVIDE? It's safer than a plain division sign, because it returns a blank instead of an error when the denominator is zero.

Now, CALCULATE, the most important function in DAX. CALCULATE evaluates a measure under modified filters. For example, Online Sales is CALCULATE of Total Sales, where the store equals "Online". Then Online Share divides Online Sales by total sales across all stores. To get that "all stores" total, it uses ALL on the store column. That ignores any store filter on the visual. Both measures are in the lesson notes.

Next, time intelligence. With a marked date table, comparisons become one-liners. Sales Last Month uses CALCULATE with DATEADD, shifting the dates back by one month. Month-on-month growth percent divides the difference between this month and last month by last month. Sales Last Year uses SAMEPERIODLASTYEAR. And Sales Financial Year To Date uses TOTALYTD, with a year-end date of "3 slash 31". That tells TOTALYTD the year ends on the thirty-first of March, which is essential for Indian financial year totals. Without it, your year-to-date resets every January. One note: Sales Last Year needs last year's data loaded, and a date table that covers it. Otherwise, it shows blank. All four measures are in the lesson notes. And remember to format percentage measures as percent, in the Measure tools ribbon.

Now let's talk about using AI to write and explain DAX. DAX is where AI help pays off most, because the logic is subtle. Always state your table and column names exactly. The first prompt in the lesson notes lists your tables and columns, and asks for a DAX measure for the percent contribution of each category to total sales, one that still shows the correct share when a Store slicer is applied. It also asks the AI to explain how filter context works in its answer. And to understand existing measures, here's the prompt: "Explain this DAX measure line by line, and tell me what it returns when a single month and a single store are selected." Then paste the measure.

Test AI-written DAX by putting the measure in a table visual, next to simple values you can verify in Excel. A measure that looks right in a card can be wrong once it's sliced by month.

What about Copilot in Power BI? Microsoft has added Copilot features to Power BI. Depending on your licence and admin settings, they can suggest DAX measures, summarise report pages in text, answer questions about the data in a model, and draft report pages from a description. The same advice applies as with Excel. Tidy, well-named models get better results, and you inspect everything it produces. Clear table and column names, like "Total Sales", not "Measure 7", help both humans and Copilot.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about twenty minutes, plus installation if needed. Pause the video and work through these steps. Step one. Install Power BI Desktop from Microsoft's site or the Microsoft Store on Windows, and load your clean sales data. Step two. Create the Date table using the DAX from the lesson notes, mark it as a date table, and relate it to Sales. Step three. Create the Total Sales, Invoices, Average Invoice Value, Sales Last Month, and Month-on-Month Growth percent measures. Step four. Add a table visual with Month Name and these measures, and verify one month's Total Sales against your Excel pivot. And step five. Ask an AI to explain CALCULATE and ALL, using your own Online Share measure as the example.

Let's recap. Power BI means load, clean with Power Query, model with relationships, measure with DAX, and visualise. Build and mark a date table, and use "3 slash 31" in TOTALYTD for Indian financial years. Use measures for anything you total or compare, and calculated columns for row-level attributes. CALCULATE changes filter context, and it's the heart of DAX. And give AI exact table and column names, then verify every measure in a table visual.
