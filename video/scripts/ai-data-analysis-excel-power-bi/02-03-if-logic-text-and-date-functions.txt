Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 2, lesson 3: IF Logic, Text and Date Functions.

Real-world sheets need decisions, like "is this invoice overdue?" They need text surgery, like "pull the state code out of the G S T I N". And they need date arithmetic, like "which financial year is this?" In this lesson, we'll cover the IF family, text functions and date functions. That's the everyday toolkit an AI can help you combine.

Let's begin with IF, AND and OR. The basic shape of IF is: a condition, then the value if true, then the value if false. For example, to flag high-value orders at Pink City Fabrics, it's equals IF, the amount is greater than or equal to ten thousand, then "High", otherwise "Regular".

You can combine conditions with AND, where all must be true, or OR, where any can be true. Say you want to flag online orders paid by cash on delivery above five thousand rupees for verification. That's an IF wrapped around an AND with three conditions. The store is "Online", the payment mode is "COD", and the amount is above five thousand. If all three are true, show "Verify". Otherwise, leave it blank.

What if you need several outcomes? Use IFS, or a nested IF. Say you want to categorise invoice size into bands. In Excel 2019 and later, Microsoft 365 and Google Sheets, IFS reads cleanly. Amounts of twenty-five thousand or more are "Large". Ten thousand or more are "Medium". Two thousand or more are "Small". And the final condition is simply TRUE, which acts as "everything else", giving "Micro". Here's the key point: IFS checks conditions in order and stops at the first true one, so put the biggest threshold first. In older Excel, you'd write the same logic as a nested IF, with each IF inside the next one. Both versions are in the lesson notes below this video.

When you inherit a monster nested IF, paste it into an AI and say, "Explain this formula as a decision table." You'll get something clear. Twenty-five thousand and above is Large. Ten thousand up to just under twenty-five thousand is Medium. Two thousand up to just under ten thousand is Small. And below two thousand is Micro. That table is also great documentation to leave in the file for the next person.

Now, a very Indian example: the G S T split, C G S T and S G S T versus I G S T. For a Rajasthan-registered seller, intra-state sales attract C G S T plus S G S T. Inter-state sales attract I G S T. Suppose the amount includes G S T, the G S T rate is in another column, and you have the customer's state. The taxable value is the amount divided by one plus the G S T rate, rounded to two decimals. Total G S T is the amount minus the taxable value. If the customer is in Rajasthan, C G S T and S G S T are each half of the total G S T, and I G S T is zero. If the customer is outside Rajasthan, all of it is I G S T. All five formulas are in the lesson notes below the video. In practice, you'd reference the taxable value column instead of repeating the calculation. And remember, this is illustrative logic for analysis. Your accountant or billing software remains the authority on actual tax treatment.

Next, text functions. Billing and CRM exports are full of text that needs trimming, splitting or standardising. Here are the main ones. TRIM removes extra spaces, so " Jaipur " with spaces becomes "Jaipur". PROPER capitalises each word, so "PINK CITY" becomes "Pink City". UPPER and LOWER change the case. LEFT gives you the first few characters, MID gives you characters from the middle, and RIGHT gives you the last few. LEN counts characters, which is great for spotting a hidden space. And SUBSTITUTE replaces text, so you can strip "Rs." out of "Rs. 1,200". There's a full table with examples in the lesson notes.

Here's a handy one. A G S T I N's first two digits are the state code. Zero eight is Rajasthan, twenty-nine is Karnataka, and twenty-seven is Maharashtra. To pull the state code out as a number, it's VALUE of LEFT of the G S T I N, two.

To split "Priya Sharma" into a first name, the classic formula uses LEFT and FIND. You find the position of the space, and take everything before it. In Microsoft 365, TEXTBEFORE and TEXTAFTER are simpler. TEXTBEFORE the cell and a space gives you the first name. In Google Sheets, SPLIT breaks text into separate cells, and REGEXEXTRACT handles harder patterns. So ask the AI for the version that matches your tool.

And to convert text like "Rs. 1,200 slash dash" into a real number, you nest SUBSTITUTE three times to remove the "Rs.", the commas and the trailing slash dash, then wrap it in VALUE. The formula is in the lesson notes.

Now, date functions. Here's the key idea. Dates in Excel are numbers, counting days since 1900, formatted to look like dates. That's why you can subtract them. Days since a lead was created is simply TODAY minus the created date.

For month labels in reports, TEXT of the date with the format "mmm dash yy" returns something like "Oct dash 26". That's good for display, but it's text, and text sorts alphabetically, so April comes before January. For grouping, prefer a real date, such as the first of the month, which you get with EOMONTH of the date, minus one, plus one.

For working days to close a deal, use NETWORKDAYS with the start date, the end date, and a locked range holding your holiday list. It excludes weekends and those holidays.

What about the Indian financial year label? For a date, you want labels like "F Y 2026 27". The logic is simple. If the month is April or later, the financial year starts in this calendar year. Otherwise, it started last year. The formula builds the label from that, and it's in the lesson notes. So the fifteenth of October 2026 gives F Y 2026 27, and the tenth of February 2027 also gives F Y 2026 27.

For the financial quarter, where Q1 is April to June, there's a clever one-liner using MOD to shift April to month zero. It works, but it's hard to read at a glance. A version anyone can audit uses CHOOSE with the month number. You simply list the quarter for each of the twelve months, January to December: Q4, Q4, Q4, Q1, Q1, Q1, Q2, Q2, Q2, Q3, Q3, Q3. Both versions are in the lesson notes. When an AI gives you a clever one-liner, it's fine to ask, "Give me a simpler version a colleague can read." Readable beats clever in shared files.

Let's recap. Use IF with AND or OR for flags, and IFS, or nested IF in old Excel, for bands, with the biggest threshold first. Ask AI to turn nested IFs into decision tables, for easy understanding and documentation. TRIM, PROPER, LEFT, MID, RIGHT, SUBSTITUTE and VALUE fix most messy text. Dates are numbers, so use EOMONTH for month starts, and a "month is four or more" test for Indian financial years. And prefer readable formulas over clever ones in shared files.
