Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 3, lesson 2: Pivot Tables and Summaries.

A pivot table turns thousands of rows into a summary in seconds. It's the backbone of almost every MIS report in India. In this lesson, we'll go from your first pivot all the way to month-on-month comparisons, with AI helping you decide what to build.

Before you pivot, get the data in shape. Pivots need a clean, tabular source. That means one header row, with every column named and no merged cells. One row per transaction, with no subtotal rows mixed in. Real dates and real numbers, as we covered in Lesson 3.1. And ideally, the data is formatted as an Excel Table, with Control T, so new rows are included when you refresh.

Now let's build your first pivot. Select the Pink City Fabrics Sales table, and follow four steps. First, go to Insert, then PivotTable, choose New Worksheet, and click OK. Second, drag Category to Rows. Third, drag Amount to Values. It should show "Sum of Amount". And fourth, drag Store to Columns. You now have sales by category and store. In Google Sheets, it's Insert, then Pivot table, and you add Rows, Columns and Values from the editor panel on the right.

A pivot has four areas. Rows groups things down the side, like category or item. Columns groups things across the top, like store or month. Values is what you calculate, like sum of amount, or count of invoice number. And Filters limits the whole pivot, for example to UPI payments only. There's a summary table in the lesson notes below this video.

Next, grouping dates by month. Drag Date to Rows. In recent Excel versions, it may group into years, quarters and months automatically. If it doesn't, right-click a date, choose Group, and select Months and Years. Always include Years, or October 2025 and October 2026 will merge into one "Oct". One more thing: Excel's grouping uses calendar quarters. For Indian financial year quarters, add a helper column in the source data, using the financial year and quarter formulas from Lesson 2.3, and use those fields instead.

Now, changing how values are shown. Right-click a value and choose Show Values As. This unlocks some of the most useful analysis. Percent of Grand Total gives each category's share of total sales. Percent of Column Total gives the category mix within each store. Difference From, with the base set to the previous month, gives the month-on-month change in rupees. Percent Difference From the previous month gives month-on-month growth as a percentage. And Running Total In Date gives cumulative sales through the year. Here's a tip. Drag Amount into Values twice. Show one as a Sum, and the other as Percent of Column Total. Then rename them by clicking the header, for example "Sales rupees" and "Share percent".

What about counting things? To count invoices, drag Invoice Number to Values, and it'll show "Count of Invoice No". But here's the catch. If an invoice has several item lines, that counts lines, not invoices. To count unique invoices in Excel on Windows, tick "Add this data to the Data Model" when you create the pivot. Then change the value setting to Distinct Count. In Google Sheets, choose COUNTUNIQUE in the "Summarise by" option.

Next, slicers and timelines. Click inside the pivot, go to PivotTable Analyze, then Insert Slicer, and choose Store and Payment Mode. You get clickable buttons that filter the pivot. That's very handy for managers who don't want to touch dropdowns. Insert Timeline does the same for dates. And one slicer can control several pivots. Just right-click the slicer and choose Report Connections.

Now, calculated fields. Want average invoice value or G S T collected inside the pivot? Go to PivotTable Analyze, then Fields, Items and Sets, then Calculated Field. For example, you could create a field named Taxable, equal to Amount divided by 1.05. But beware. Calculated fields operate on sums, not individual rows. That's fine for a single G S T rate. But with mixed five percent and twelve percent items, it gives wrong results. Instead, add a Taxable column to the source data, and pivot that. This is a trap AI assistants sometimes walk into. So if a suggestion uses calculated fields with ratios or mixed rates, double-check it.

Let's talk about using AI to design the pivot. Often you know the business question, but not which fields to drag where. So describe both. The prompt in the lesson notes lists your columns: date, store, category, item, quantity, amount and payment mode. It says your manager wants to know which categories drove the growth from September to October 2026, and whether that differed between the Online channel and the physical stores. Then it asks exactly how to set up the pivot, meaning rows, columns, values, filters and Show Values As, and what to look for in the result. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. A sensible answer would be: Category in Rows, Date grouped by Month in Columns, Store as a slicer, and Amount shown as Difference From the previous month. Then sort by the October difference, largest first, to see the growth drivers.

Now, formula-based summaries versus pivots. Pivots are fast to build and explore. SUMIFS grids, from Lesson 2.2, give fixed layouts that don't shift when the data changes. That's better for a report template that must look identical every month. Many MIS reports use both: pivots to explore, and SUMIFS for the final printed format. In Excel 365, you can also reference pivot values reliably with GETPIVOTDATA. It's created automatically when you click a pivot cell while writing a formula.

Google Sheets users have a third option, the QUERY function. It lets you write something like a database query: select the category, sum the amount, group by category, and order by the total, largest first. The final argument tells QUERY there's one header row. It produces a live summary table that updates as the data changes. The formula is in the lesson notes below the video.

And finally: refresh, refresh, refresh. The most common pivot mistake is adding new data and forgetting to refresh. Right-click the pivot and choose Refresh, or go to Data, then Refresh All, for every pivot in the workbook. If your source is a Table, new rows are picked up automatically on refresh. But if it's a fixed range, like A1 to K1000, rows beyond 1000 are silently ignored.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about twenty minutes, so pause the video and work through these steps. Step one. Use your cleaned practice data from Lesson 3.1, or ask an AI for one hundred rows of Pink City Fabrics sales across August to October 2026. Step two. Build a pivot with Category in Rows, Date grouped by Month and Year in Columns, and Sum of Amount in Values. Step three. Add a second copy of Amount, shown as Percent Difference From the previous month. Step four. Add slicers for Store and Payment Mode. And step five. Write two sentences: which category grew most, and in which channel. Then ask an AI to suggest one follow-up question your manager might ask.

Let's recap. Pivots need clean, tabular data with real dates and numbers, ideally in an Excel Table. Group dates by month and year, and use helper columns for Indian financial year quarters. Show Values As gives you shares, month-on-month differences and running totals without formulas. Avoid calculated fields for row-level maths like mixed G S T rates. Add a source column instead. And always refresh after new data arrives.
