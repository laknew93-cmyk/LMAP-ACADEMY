Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 4, lesson 3: Copilot in Excel and Gemini in Google Sheets.

Microsoft and Google have built AI directly into their spreadsheets. Instead of copying data into a chat window, you can ask questions right inside the workbook. In this lesson, you'll learn what these built-in assistants are good at, where they struggle, and how to work with them effectively.

First, a note on availability. Copilot in Excel and Gemini in Google Sheets are evolving quickly. What you can access depends on your licence, your organisation's admin settings and your region. Some features need the file saved to OneDrive or SharePoint. Some need a particular business or personal plan. And some appear first in preview. So rather than memorising menus that will move, learn the kinds of things these tools do, and how to ask for them. If you don't see the feature at work, ask your IT team. Many companies switch it on only for certain users.

So what does built-in spreadsheet AI typically do? Both Copilot in Excel and Gemini in Sheets broadly offer seven things. They suggest formulas and new columns, for example, "Add a column with the G S T amount, assuming the amount includes G S T at the rate in column J." They highlight, sort and filter, like "Highlight invoices above ten thousand rupees in orange." They summarise and create pivots or charts, like "Show total sales by category and store as a PivotTable." They answer questions about the data, like "Which store had the highest average invoice value in August?" They spot trends and outliers, like "Are there any unusual values in the Amount column?" They generate tables or templates, like "Create a monthly expense tracker for a small CA office." And they explain formulas, like "Explain the formula in F2." There's a table of these in the lesson notes below this video. Some Excel plans can also run Python-based analysis inside the workbook, which Copilot can use for deeper work like forecasting. Check what your version actually offers.

Here's the big advantage: context. The built-in assistants can see your sheet's structure. That's the column headers, the data types, and the selected range. That removes much of the "describe your sheet" work from Lesson 1.2. And their output lands directly in your workbook, as a real formula, pivot or chart that you can inspect and keep.

Now, preparing your sheet for built-in AI. These tools work best on tidy data. So before you ask anything, do four things. One, format your data as a Table in Excel, with Control T. Copilot typically works best on data in a Table or a clearly structured range. Two, use clear, unique headers. "Amount incl GST", not "Amt" or a blank. Three, have one header row, no merged cells, and no blank rows or columns in the middle. And four, use real dates and numbers. The cleaning from Module 3 still matters. If Copilot or Gemini says it can't understand your data, the problem is almost always structure.

Let's look at how to phrase requests. The prompting principles are the same. Be specific about columns, conditions and output. A vague request is just "Analyse sales." A better one is: "Create a PivotTable on a new sheet showing total amount including G S T by category in rows and month in columns, for July to September 2026, sorted by September sales, highest first."

For formulas, try: "Add a column called FY Quarter that returns Q1 for April to June, Q2 for July to September, Q3 for October to December, and Q4 for January to March, based on the Date column."

And for quick insight: "What are the three biggest changes in sales by category between August and September? Show the numbers."

Next, always inspect the output. Treat a suggested formula exactly as you would one from a chat assistant. Read the formula before accepting it. Does it reference the right columns? Spot-check two or three rows by hand. For pivots and charts, check the fields used, and whether filters were applied. And for "insights", ask for the numbers behind them, as in Lesson 4.2. When the assistant offers an Insert or Add to sheet button, preview, test, and then insert. Undo, with Control Z, and version history on OneDrive or Google Drive are your safety net.

So where do built-in assistants struggle? There are five main areas. First, messy layouts, like reports with merged headers, subtotal rows, or several tables on one sheet. Second, multi-sheet logic. Questions that need joining several tables may not work as smoothly as a single-table question, so you may still need XLOOKUP or Power Query. Third, company-specific definitions. It doesn't know your firm's definition of "active customer" or "net sales" unless you spell it out. Fourth, very large or complex workbooks, where performance and accuracy can drop. And fifth, reproducibility. Asking the same question twice may produce slightly different approaches. So for recurring reports, convert the result into fixed formulas and pivots.

Now, how do you choose between built-in AI, a chat assistant, and doing it yourself? For a quick formula or highlight on a tidy table, use the built-in assistant. For understanding a complex legacy formula, either works, though chat assistants give longer explanations. For exploratory analysis of an exported file, use a chat assistant with data analysis, or the built-in Python features. For sensitive data with no approved tool, do it yourself with formulas and pivots. For a monthly recurring report, build it with formulas, pivots and Power Query, and use AI to help design it. And for learning a function for the long term, ask for an explanation, then type it yourself. The comparison table is in the lesson notes. Here's the thing: if you only ever click Insert, you'll struggle when the AI is unavailable, or wrong.

Let's look at an example from a Chennai CA firm. Meena is an article assistant there. She receives a client's expense register in Excel, with six hundred rows, and columns for date, vendor, ledger head, amount and G S T input. Using Copilot on the Table, she first asks: "Show total amount and G S T input by ledger head for Q2 F Y 2026 27, that's July to September 2026, as a PivotTable, sorted by amount, descending." Then she asks: "Highlight rows where G S T input is more than eighteen percent of amount."

Why eighteen percent? In this register, the amount is the taxable value, and most of the client's routine expenses attract G S T at eighteen percent or less. So a higher ratio is worth a closer look. It could be a data-entry error, or a legitimately higher-rated item. Meena then reviews each highlighted row herself. The AI found candidates. The professional judgement stays with her.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about fifteen minutes. If you don't have Copilot or Gemini in your spreadsheet, do the same steps with a chat assistant by pasting fifteen sample rows. Pause the video and follow along. Step one. Open your clean Pink City Fabrics Sales table, or any tidy table of a hundred rows or more. Step two. Ask the built-in assistant to add a Taxable column. Read the formula, and test it on three rows. Step three. Ask it to create a pivot of sales by category and month. Check the grand total against SUM of the amount column. Step four. Ask, "What's one unusual pattern in this data?" Then ask for the numbers and row count behind it. And step five. Note one thing it did well, and one thing you had to correct.

Let's recap. Copilot in Excel and Gemini in Sheets add formulas, pivots, charts and Q and A inside your file, and availability depends on your licence and admin settings. They work best on tidy Tables, with clear headers and real data types. Be specific in your requests, and inspect every formula, pivot and insight before relying on it. They struggle with messy layouts, multi-table logic and company-specific definitions. And use them to speed up your work, but keep learning the underlying functions yourself.
