Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 5, lesson 3: Capstone: Write a Five-Insight Executive Summary.

Numbers don't change decisions. Clear sentences do. In this final project, you'll learn to ask sharper analytical questions, turn your findings into a story, and write a one-page, five-insight executive summary that a busy director will actually read.

Let's start by moving from "what happened" to "so what". Most reports stop at description, like "Sales were fifty-one lakh rupees in September." That's useful, but the reader still has to work out what it means. Strong analysis moves through four levels. Level one is describe: what happened? For example, September sales were fifty-one lakh rupees, up eight percent on August. Level two is diagnose: why did it happen? Almost all of the increase came from Kurtis, mainly through Online. Level three is assess: does it matter? Online Kurtis are now a meaningful share of sales, and cash on delivery is high in that segment. And level four is recommend: what should we do? Stock up on Kurtis for Diwali, and trial a small prepaid discount online to reduce cash on delivery. Your executive summary should reach at least "assess" for every insight, and "recommend" for most of them.

Next, asking better analytical questions. Weak questions get weak insights. So upgrade them with six patterns. First, compare, don't just count. Not "what were sales?", but "how do sales compare with last month, last year and target?" Second, decompose the change. "Sales rose four lakh rupees. Which stores, categories and items explain that four lakh?" Third, split the average. Averages hide things. "Average invoice value is flat" may hide one store rising and another falling. Fourth, look for concentration. "What share of sales comes from the top ten items, or the top twenty customers?" Fifth, check the rate, not just the volume. For LeadLoop, more leads from IndiaMART is only good if the win rate holds. And sixth, ask what's missing. "Are there stores, months or categories with zero sales that should have some?"

Use AI as a thinking partner to generate questions before you analyse. The prompt in the lesson notes describes the analysis, Q2 sales for a Jaipur clothing retailer ahead of Diwali stock planning, and lists the data columns. Then it asks for ten analytical questions that would most help the owner decide what to stock and where to focus, and for each one, which columns and which comparison you'd need. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. Then choose the six to eight most decision-relevant questions, and answer them with your own pivots, formulas, or verified AI analysis.

So what makes an insight worth including? Run each candidate finding through four filters. One, is it true? Has it been verified with the checks from Lesson 4.2? Two, is it material? A fifty percent rise from ten thousand rupees to fifteen thousand rupees might not matter in a fifty-lakh business. So express effects in rupees, not just percentages. Three, is it new? "Saree sales rise before festivals" is known to everyone in Jaipur. But "Saree sales rose less than last year" is news. And four, is it actionable? Can someone do something differently because of it? Keep the five that pass best. And if you only have three strong insights, write three. Padding weakens the whole document.

Now, the structure of a one-page summary. There's a template in the lesson notes. At the top, a title with the business and the period, plus the date, who prepared it, and the data source with its "as of" date. Then a headline of one or two sentences, giving the single most important message. Then a small table of key numbers: sales, month-on-month percent, versus target, invoices, and average invoice value. Then five insights. Each one has a bold one-line headline stating the finding, two or three sentences of evidence with numbers in rupees and percent, and one sentence of implication or suggested action. And finally, data notes, covering the period, exclusions, G S T treatment and known limitations.

Here's an important point. Insight headlines should be complete sentences that state the point, not topic labels. Compare these two. A weak headline is "Category performance". A strong one is "Kurtis drove nearly all of September's growth, almost entirely online." A reader who only scans the bold lines should still get the full story.

Next, writing with AI, without losing the truth. AI is excellent at turning your verified findings into crisp prose. The key is to give it your numbers, and forbid it from adding new ones. The prompt in the lesson notes asks for a one-page executive summary for the owner of Pink City Fabrics for Q2 of the financial year. It says to use only the verified facts listed, and not to add any numbers, causes or claims that aren't listed. If something seems to need more evidence, it should say "to be confirmed" instead of guessing. It specifies the structure, a direct, plain-English tone, and amounts in lakh rupees with one decimal. Then you list your verified facts and data notes. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

After the draft comes back, do four things. First, check every number against your fact list. Any number that isn't in your list must go. Second, check the causal language. "Because of the influencer campaign" is a claim. If you didn't verify it, change it to "coinciding with", or remove it. Third, cut twenty percent. First drafts are always too long, and executives reward brevity. And fourth, read it aloud. If a sentence is awkward to say, it's awkward to read.

Let's look at a worked example. This one's illustrative, using made-up figures from the practice dataset. The headline is: "Online cash on delivery is rising, and now accounts for over half of online orders." The evidence says the cash on delivery share of online invoices rose from forty-one percent in July to fifty-four percent in September, while online sales grew eighteen percent. Return-to-origin data isn't in this dataset, so the cost impact is to be confirmed. And the suggested action is: pull the return-to-origin data from the courier dashboard, and if cash on delivery returns are high, test a small prepaid incentive in October. Notice three things. There are specific numbers. There's honesty about what the data can't show. And there's a proportionate next step, rather than a sweeping recommendation.

Finally, presenting it. Send the summary as the email body, or as a one-page PDF, with the dashboard or MIS workbook attached for anyone who wants detail. Lead the email with the headline sentence, because many readers will stop there. In a meeting, talk through the five bold lines, and let the evidence support the questions. And end with the decisions you need. For example: "Approve additional Kurti stock of X lakh rupees by the twenty-fifth of October?"

Now, try it yourself. This capstone takes forty-five to sixty minutes, so pause the video and work through these steps. Step one. Use your clean Pink City Fabrics data, or LeadLoop's leads, or a masked work dataset. Step two. Ask an AI for ten analytical questions, choose six to eight, and answer them with pivots or verified analysis. Step three. Filter your findings through the four tests: true, material, new and actionable, and keep the best five. Step four. Write your verified facts list, then use the prompt from this lesson to get a draft. Step five. Edit the draft. Check every number, fix any causal claims, and cut twenty percent. And step six. Save the final one-pager alongside your dashboard and MIS report. Together, they form your portfolio from this course.

Let's recap. Move every insight from describe, to diagnose, to assess, and recommend where you can. Ask comparative, decomposing questions, and let AI help you brainstorm them. Keep only insights that are true, material, new and actionable, five or fewer. Write bold, complete-sentence headlines, so a scanner gets the whole story. And give AI only verified facts, forbid new numbers, and edit hard before sending.
