Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 4, lesson 2: Verifying AI Analysis Before You Share It.

An AI-generated number that's wrong is worse than no number at all, because it looks authoritative. In this lesson, you'll get a practical verification system, so you can use AI's speed without staking your reputation on unchecked figures.

Let's start with how AI analysis goes wrong. With real business data, the errors are rarely bad arithmetic. They're usually one of eight things. First, misparsed dates. Sales appear in months when there was no business, because "05 dash 08 dash 2026" was read as the eighth of May instead of the fifth of August. Second, wrong grain. Averages come out too low, and counts too high, because average "order" value was computed per item line. Third, silent filtering. Totals are slightly low, because rows with a blank store were dropped without any mention. Fourth, duplicates included. Totals are slightly high, because re-exported rows were counted twice. Fifth, the wrong definition. A ratio differs from your company's, like conversion calculated as won over all leads, instead of won over closed. Sixth, units confusion. Numbers are off by a hundred thousand, because of lakhs versus rupees, or G S T-inclusive versus taxable. Seventh, invented numbers, like a chat reply quoting totals without running any code. And eighth, plausible over-reading, meaning strong claims from weak patterns, like "Tuesdays drive sales", based on twelve data points. The full table is in the lesson notes below this video.

Here's the thing. Your job isn't to redo everything. It's to run quick checks that would catch each of these. So let's go through the five-check routine.

Check one: control totals. Before trusting any breakdown, confirm that the grand total and the row count match an independent source. For row count in Excel, use ROWS of the Sales table, or the count in the status bar. For the grand total, use SUM of the amount column. Then ask the AI for both. If they don't match, stop, and resolve it before going any further. For finance-related data, also compare against the source system. That might be the billing software's monthly sales register, or the G S T R 1 summary your accountant prepared. Small, explainable differences, like cancelled invoices or credit notes, are normal. But you need to be able to explain them.

Check two: spot-check one cell by hand. Pick one number from the AI's table. Say, Kurtis, Vaishali store, August: one lakh, eighty-four thousand, two hundred and fifty rupees. Filter your Excel table for exactly those conditions, and look at the Sum in the status bar. Or write a SUMIFS for that category, store and date range. The formula is in the lesson notes. If one random cell matches, the logic is probably right. If it doesn't, the whole table is suspect.

Check three: parts add up to the whole. Breakdowns should sum to the total. Category totals should add up to monthly totals, and percentage shares should add up to one hundred percent. If the AI's category table sums to forty-eight point two lakh, but total sales are fifty-one lakh, some rows were dropped. Often that's blanks, or an unexpected spelling.

Check four: a sense check against the business. Ask yourself three questions. Is this plausible, given what I know? A three hundred percent jump in Bedsheets in one month, for a stable retailer, needs an explanation, maybe a bulk hotel order, before it goes in a report. Are the signs right? Returns should reduce sales, not increase them. And are the magnitudes right? A two-store Jaipur retailer with fifty crore rupees in monthly sales would be very unusual. That's probably a units error.

Check five: re-ask from a different angle. Ask the same question a different way, or in a fresh chat, and see if you get the same answer. For example, ask "What's total Online cash on delivery amount in September?" And separately, ask "Show payment mode by month for Online only." If the two disagree, dig in.

Now, let's talk about verifying claims, not just numbers. AI assistants also produce interpretations, like "Online growth is driven by Bengaluru customers." Treat each claim as a hypothesis, and ask four questions. First, what's the evidence? Say, "Show me the numbers behind that statement." Second, how big is the effect? "Bengaluru online sales grew forty percent" might mean twelve thousand rupees became sixteen thousand eight hundred. That's not material for the business. Third, could it be chance? With small samples, a few dozen orders, patterns appear and vanish from month to month. So ask, "How many orders is this based on?" And fourth, correlation versus cause. "Customers paying by UPI spend more" doesn't mean pushing UPI will raise bill sizes.

Here's a good follow-up prompt: "For each insight you just gave me, show: the exact numbers it's based on, the number of rows or orders behind it, and one alternative explanation I should rule out. Mark any insight based on fewer than thirty orders as low confidence." Now, thirty is a rough rule of thumb, not a statistical law. The point is to make the sample size visible.

Next, keep an audit trail. When AI-assisted analysis goes into a report, keep a short note with it. The example in the lesson notes records five things. The name of the analysis. The source file, with its row count and total. The tool used, and the date. The checks you ran, like control total, spot-check and parts equal whole, each ticked. And your assumptions, like how duplicates were removed, whether amounts include G S T, and which rows were excluded. It takes two minutes, and it protects you completely if someone questions the numbers later.

So when should you rebuild instead of verify? If a number will be used for something consequential, like a bonus calculation, a board deck, a tax figure or a pricing decision, don't rely on the AI's output at all. Use the AI to find the insight, then rebuild the key figures in your own workbook, with formulas or pivots you control. The AI was your scout. Your spreadsheet is the evidence.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about fifteen minutes, so pause the video and work through these steps. Step one. Take the AI analysis you did in Lesson 4.1, or run a quick one now on your project file. Step two. Run all five checks. Control totals, one spot-check using SUMIFS, parts adding up to the whole, a sense check, and a re-ask from a different angle. Step three. Deliberately test the date trap. Upload a small file with day-month-year text dates, without mentioning the format. Does the AI parse them correctly? Ask it to show the minimum and maximum date. Step four. Use the "evidence, sample size, alternative explanation" prompt on the AI's insights. And step five. Write an audit-trail note for your analysis.

Let's recap. AI analysis errors are usually logic errors, involving dates, grain, filtering and definitions, not arithmetic. Always match control totals and row counts before trusting any breakdown. One SUMIFS spot-check and a parts-sum-to-whole check catch most problems. Treat AI interpretations as hypotheses, and ask for evidence, sample size and alternatives. And for consequential numbers, rebuild them in your own workbook, and keep an audit note.
