Welcome to AI for Data Analysis, module 4, lesson 1: Analysing C S V Files with ChatGPT and Claude.

Several chat assistants can now take an uploaded spreadsheet, run real calculations on it, and return tables and charts. In this lesson, you'll learn to use that capability well. What to upload, what to ask, and how to make the AI show its working.

First, what's actually happening when you upload a file? When you upload a C S V or Excel file to an assistant with a data analysis feature, the assistant typically writes and runs code, usually Python, in a sandbox, to read and analyse the file. ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini all offer some version of this, depending on your plan and settings. And that's very different from the AI just "reading" the numbers and guessing.

Here's why that matters. First, code-based analysis usually means accurate arithmetic. Sums and averages are calculated, not estimated. Second, the logic can still be wrong. If the code reads dates as month first, or counts item lines instead of invoices, the arithmetic will be perfectly accurate, and the answer will still be wrong. And third, you can inspect the code. Most tools let you expand a "show work" or code section. Even if you don't know Python, the comments and variable names tell you a lot. So if an assistant answers questions about your file without running any code, treat the numbers as rough at best. It may be estimating from a preview of the data.

Next, prepare the file. There are five things to do. One, mask and minimise, as we covered in Lesson 1.3. Delete unnecessary columns from a copy. Two, keep one clean table per sheet, with headers in row 1, and no merged cells or subtotal rows. Three, C S V is the safest format. Excel files work too, but multiple sheets, hidden rows and formatting can confuse things. Use File, Save As, and choose C S V U T F 8. Four, use clear column names. "Amount incl GST" beats "Amt2". And five, mention size. Very large files may hit upload limits. For big data, aggregate first, for example monthly totals by store, or use Power BI or Python locally.

Now, start with orientation, not questions. Your first prompt should make the AI describe the data back to you. That catches misreads before they contaminate every answer. The orientation prompt in the lesson notes tells the AI what the data is, then asks for four things before any analysis. The number of rows and columns, with each column's detected data type. The minimum and maximum of the date and amount. The distinct values of store, category and payment mode, with counts. And anything that looks like a data quality problem. It also states that dates are day-month-year, Indian format, and that amounts are in rupees including G S T. And it ends with "Don't analyse further yet." You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video.

Then check the reply against what you know. If it says the dates run from January to December, when you know they're July to September, it's parsed day-month as month-day. Correct it right away: "Parse Date as day-first."

Once orientation looks right, ask business questions, one at a time. Specific beats broad. Instead of "Analyse this data", ask "Show total amount by month and store as a table, with months in rows." Instead of "Any insights?", ask "Which three categories had the biggest rupee increase from July to September? Show the numbers." And instead of "Is cash on delivery a problem?", ask "For Online orders only, show cash on delivery's share of invoices and of amount, by month." The comparison is in the lesson notes.

Ask for tables first, and charts second. Tables let you verify the numbers. A chart can hide an error behind a nice-looking line.

There are some useful question patterns in the lesson notes. For example: break down the change in total sales from July to September by category, and say which categories explain most of the change. Compare average invoice value by store, calculating invoice totals first by grouping on invoice number. Find which day of the week has the highest average sales per store. Identify invoices whose amount is more than three times the median for their category. And create a pivot-style table with category in rows, payment mode in columns, and percent of row total. Notice that instruction, "calculate invoice totals first". You're explicitly steering the grain issue from Lesson 3.3.

Next, make it show its working. Build three habits into your prompts. Say, "Show the code you ran," even if you only skim it. Say, "State any assumptions you made, such as how you handled blanks or duplicates." And say, "Give me the row count used for this calculation." Row counts are a powerful check. If the AI says it analysed one thousand rows, but you removed twenty-five duplicates and should have nine hundred and seventy-five, something's off.

Now, getting outputs you can use. Ask for results in forms you can take back to Excel. For example: "Export the category by month summary as a C S V I can download." Or: "Give me the Excel formula I would use to reproduce this number in my own workbook, assuming my data is in a Table called Sales." That second prompt is underrated. It moves the analysis into your controlled, refreshable workbook, and gives you an independent check at the same time.

For charts, ask for a specific chart type, with a clear title, labelled axes and rupee formatting. Many tools let you download the chart as an image for a quick email. But for recurring reports, you'll want charts built in Excel, Sheets or Power BI, which we cover in Module 5.

The same approach works for the Bengaluru leads sheet, LeadLoop's CRM export. The prompt in the lesson notes describes the data and its columns, then asks three things. For each source, show leads, won, lost, the win rate, defined as won divided by won plus lost, and total won deal value. For won deals, show the median days from created to closed, by source. And find which owner has the most leads stuck in the Demo stage for more than thirty days as of the fifteenth of October 2026. You'll find the full prompt in the lesson notes below this video. Notice that defining win rate in the prompt stops the AI picking a different definition, like won divided by all leads.

So where are uploads not the right tool? There are four cases. Recurring reports, because you don't want to re-upload and re-prompt every month. Build those in Excel, Sheets or Power BI. Sensitive data that your organisation hasn't approved for external tools. Very large datasets, where you should aggregate first, or use Power BI or Python on your own machine. And final numbers for statutory or financial filings, where your accounting system is the source of truth. Think of uploads as a fast, smart first pass and a second opinion, not the system of record.

Now, try it yourself. This takes about twenty minutes, so pause the video and follow along. Step one. Take your cleaned project file from Lesson 3.3, and save the Sales table as a C S V. Step two. Upload it to an assistant with data analysis. Run the orientation prompt, and check every detail. Step three. Ask the five project questions from Lesson 3.3, one at a time, requesting tables and row counts. Step four. Compare at least three numbers with your pivot results, and note any differences. And step five. Ask for the Excel formula to reproduce one of the answers, and test it in your workbook.

Let's recap. Data analysis features usually run real code. The arithmetic is reliable, but the logic still needs checking. Start with an orientation prompt, so the AI describes the data back before analysing. Ask specific questions, request tables before charts, and define metrics like win rate yourself. Ask for code, assumptions and row counts every time. And use uploads for exploration and second opinions, but build recurring reports in your own tools.
