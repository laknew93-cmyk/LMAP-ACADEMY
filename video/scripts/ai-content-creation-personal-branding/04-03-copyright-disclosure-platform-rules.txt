Welcome to AI for Content Creation and Personal Branding, module 4, lesson 3: Copyright, AI Disclosure and Platform Rules.

The fastest way to damage a brand you've spent years building is a copyright complaint, a misleading sponsored post, or a viral deepfake controversy. This lesson covers the rules creators in India need to understand. Copyright, AI disclosure, influencer advertising guidelines, and platform policies. It's practical guidance, not legal advice. When money or serious risk is involved, talk to a qualified lawyer.

Let's start with copyright basics for creators. Copyright protects original creative works. Writing, photos, music, videos, artwork and software. In India, this is governed by the Copyright Act of nineteen fifty-seven. There are four practical principles. First, other people's content isn't free to use just because it's online. Images from Google, songs, film clips and other creators' videos are usually protected. Second, credit isn't permission. Writing "credit to the owner" doesn't make using someone's photo or video legal. Third, music is the most common trap. Use music from your editor's licensed library or the platform's own audio library. And remember that licences can be limited to that platform. And fourth, fonts, templates and stock images have licences too. Check whether commercial use is allowed, especially for client work.

Now, what about AI and copyright? The legal position on AI-generated content is still evolving, in India and around the world. Keep four points in mind. First, ownership of purely AI-generated output is uncertain. Courts and copyright offices in different countries have taken different views on whether such works can be protected, and who owns them. Your own human contribution, like writing, editing, selection and arrangement, strengthens your claim. Second, tool terms of service matter. Read what each AI tool says about who owns the output, and how it can be used commercially. Third, don't prompt for copies. Asking a generator to reproduce a specific artist's work, a film character, a brand logo or another creator's distinctive content invites infringement problems. And fourth, don't paste other people's articles into AI and publish the rewrite. It adds nothing, and it can still infringe.

Next, disclosure of AI-generated content. Platforms and governments are moving towards labelling synthetic content, especially realistic images, audio and video. As of now, YouTube asks creators to disclose when realistic content has been meaningfully altered or synthetically generated, and it may add a label. Meta platforms, meaning Instagram and Facebook, have introduced "AI info" style labels, and ask users to label realistic AI-generated video or audio. Other platforms have their own rules and labels. And the Indian government has issued advisories, and has been working on rules under the I T Rules to require labelling of synthetically generated information.

These policies change. So check the current help pages of each platform you use, and follow any news on India's I T Rules.

Here's a sensible personal policy, regardless of the minimum rules. If AI helped you draft or edit a post you reviewed, disclosure generally isn't required. It's your choice. If it's a clearly stylised AI illustration, like a cartoon or abstract image, a label isn't usually required, but it's optional. But you should disclose a realistic AI image of a person, place or event. You should disclose a cloned voice or AI avatar presenting content. AI dubbing of your own video into another language deserves a short note, as good practice. And altered real footage that could mislead must be disclosed, and you should think twice before publishing it at all. The table is in the lesson notes below this video.

Now let's talk about influencer and sponsored content rules. If you earn from brand deals, disclosure is mandatory, not optional. In India, the Advertising Standards Council of India, or A S C I, has guidelines for influencer advertising. And the Department of Consumer Affairs has issued endorsement guidelines for social media influencers. In essence, there are six things to follow. First, disclose any material connection with a brand. That includes payment, free products, discounts, and family or employment relationships. Second, make disclosure prominent and clear. Use labels like Ad, Sponsored, Collaboration or Partnership at the start, not hidden among hashtags at the end. Third, for videos, the disclosure should appear in the video itself, not only in the caption. Fourth, don't endorse products you haven't used, or claims you can't support. Fifth, be especially careful in sensitive categories like finance, health and education. A S C I has additional expectations for creators giving advice on things like financial products and health. And sixth, A S C I also expects disclosure when a virtual or AI-generated influencer is used.

Read the current guidelines directly on the A S C I website before taking on paid work.

Next, platform rules that affect AI content. Most platforms prohibit or restrict five things. Impersonation of real people or brands. Misleading synthetic media, especially around elections, health and finance. Fake engagement, like bought followers, bots, comment pods and automated AI comments. Spam, meaning mass-produced, repetitive or low-value content. And misleading thumbnails and titles.

Automating engagement with AI, like posting bulk AI comments on others' posts, can lead to restricted reach or suspended accounts.

Finally, let's protect your own content. Watermark or brand your carousels and graphics subtly. Keep your original files and drafts as proof of creation. Use platform reporting tools if someone copies your work. And be aware that public content may be scraped for AI training. Some platforms provide settings related to this, so review your privacy settings.

Read A S C I's current influencer guidelines summary, and note the disclosure labels you'll use. And step five. Add a pre-publish checklist to your brand voice guide. Rights checked, facts checked, AI disclosure decided, and sponsorship disclosed.

Let's recap. Online content isn't free to use. Credit isn't permission, and music licences are a frequent trap. The law on ownership of AI-generated content is unsettled, so your human contribution and the tool's terms matter. Label realistic synthetic images, voices and videos, and follow each platform's current rules. Disclose every brand relationship clearly and prominently, as the A S C I and consumer affairs guidelines require. And avoid impersonation, fake engagement and spammy mass-produced content.
