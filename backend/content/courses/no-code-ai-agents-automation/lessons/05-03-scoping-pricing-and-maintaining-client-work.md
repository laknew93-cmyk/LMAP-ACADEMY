Getting a "yes" from a client is half the job. The other half is scoping clearly, pricing sensibly, handing over properly and earning ongoing income from maintenance. This final lesson gives you the templates and habits to run automation projects professionally.

## Scope tightly

Most automation projects that go badly were scoped vaguely. "Automate our sales process" has no end. "When a lead submits the website form, score it with AI, save it to the Leads sheet, alert the owner on WhatsApp for HOT leads, and send an approved WhatsApp template within five minutes" has a clear finish line.

A good scope document covers:

```text
1. Goal: the business outcome in one sentence
2. In scope: each workflow, its trigger, its steps, its outputs
3. Out of scope: what you are NOT building (be explicit)
4. Client provides: account access, AI provider account + key,
   WhatsApp provider account, sample data, a contact person
5. Tools and running costs: which platforms, who pays for them
6. Data handling: what data flows where, which AI provider processes it
7. Testing and acceptance: the test set, what "done" means
8. Timeline: milestones with dates
9. Handover: documentation, a walkthrough session
10. Support: what's included after launch and for how long
```

The "out of scope" and "client provides" sections prevent most disputes. Delays usually come from waiting for access, not from building.

## Pricing approaches

There is no standard price for automation work, and rates vary widely by city, niche, experience and client size. Here are common structures; choose what fits and adjust as you learn.

| Model | How it works | Good for |
|---|---|---|
| Fixed project fee | One price for a scoped build | Clear, well-defined workflows |
| Setup + monthly retainer | Build fee, then a monthly fee for monitoring, fixes and small changes | Most SMB clients; creates recurring income |
| Hourly or daily rate | Bill time spent | Discovery, audits, unclear scope |
| Value-based | Price tied to the value created | Experienced builders with proven results |

### Thinking about the number

Rather than copying someone's rate card, reason it out:

- **Your time:** estimate build, testing, meetings and handover hours honestly – then add a buffer, because access and revisions always take longer.
- **The value to the client:** if the workflow saves a team many hours a month or speeds up lead response, your fee should feel small next to that value.
- **The client's size:** a three-person clinic and a 200-person manufacturer can afford different amounts for the same workflow.

Purely as an illustration of structure (not a benchmark): a beginner might quote a modest fixed fee for a single lead workflow plus a small monthly retainer, while an experienced builder with case studies charges several times that for a multi-workflow system. Your rates will depend on your market and results; raise them as your proof grows.

### Running costs are separate

Make it clear in writing that tool subscriptions, AI usage and WhatsApp messaging charges are paid by the client directly to the providers (or itemised if you pass them through). Mention that these costs can vary with volume and provider pricing changes.

### GST and invoicing

If you are registered under GST, your invoices need to follow GST rules; whether you need to register depends on your turnover and type of supply. Speak to a CA about your situation, and use a simple written agreement for every client.

## Payment terms

Common, fair practice for project work: an advance before starting (often a portion such as half), the balance on acceptance. Retainers are billed monthly in advance. Accept UPI or bank transfer and send a proper invoice each time. Don't start building before the advance lands – it protects both of you from casual commitments.

## Handover done right

At the end of the project, deliver:

- **The one-page workflow document** for each workflow (from the previous lesson).
- **A short screen-recorded walkthrough:** what it does, where to see logs, what to do if an alert arrives.
- **Ownership confirmation:** workflows live in the client's account (or a clearly agreed account), all credentials are in the client's name, and you have access only as a team member they can remove.
- **The test set**, so future changes can be checked.

Building inside the client's own accounts from day one makes handover trivial and builds trust. Avoid locking clients in by keeping their workflows in your personal account – it may feel like leverage but it damages reputation.

## Maintenance and retainers

Workflows need care: apps update, forms change, AI models are retired and replaced, prompts need tuning as the business changes. A monthly retainer can include:

- Monitoring alerts and fixing failures within an agreed response time.
- A monthly health check: review error logs, AI quality samples and costs.
- A fixed number of small changes per month (e.g. new label, new form field).
- A short monthly summary for the owner: runs, errors, items needing review, suggestions.

Define response times realistically – "within one working day" is more honest than "24/7" for a solo freelancer.

## Growing beyond the first project

The best source of your next project is your current client. After a successful lead workflow, the natural next steps are often an FAQ bot, inbox triage or a weekly report. Use the monthly summary to suggest one improvement at a time. Ask satisfied clients for a referral or a short written testimonial in their own words.

## Ethics and trust

A few principles that keep you in business long-term:

- Tell end customers when they are talking to a bot, and offer a human.
- Never automate spam, scraped contact lists or messages without consent.
- Keep humans in control of money, legal and medical decisions.
- Be honest about what AI can't do. Under-promise, over-deliver.

## Try it yourself

Spend 20 minutes preparing your first proposal:

1. Pick one project you built in this course and imagine a specific client for it.
2. Fill in the 10-section scope template above.
3. Decide a pricing model and write a short justification based on your estimated hours and the client's likely value.
4. Draft a one-paragraph retainer offer listing what's included each month.
5. Ask a friend who runs a business to read it and tell you what is unclear.

## Key takeaways

- Tight scope with explicit "out of scope" and "client provides" sections prevents most disputes.
- Choose a pricing model (fixed, setup plus retainer, hourly, value-based) and reason from time, value and client size.
- Running costs belong to the client; take advice from a CA on GST and invoicing.
- Build in the client's accounts and hand over documentation, a walkthrough and the test set.
- Maintenance retainers create recurring income and keep workflows healthy.
