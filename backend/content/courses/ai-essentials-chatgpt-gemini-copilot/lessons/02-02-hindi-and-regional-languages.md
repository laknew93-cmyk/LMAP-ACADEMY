Much of India's work happens in more than one language. A shop owner might write to suppliers in Hindi, to customers in Marathi and to the bank in English. This lesson shows you how to use AI assistants for drafting, translating and switching tone across Indian languages.

## What AI can do with Indian languages

The major assistants can read and write in Hindi and many other Indian languages, including Bengali, Marathi, Tamil, Telugu, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam and Punjabi. Quality varies by language and by tool — results in Hindi are generally stronger than in languages with less online text — but for everyday drafting they are often very useful.

Common uses:

- Drafting a customer message in Hindi or a regional language
- Translating an English circular for staff who prefer their own language
- Writing in Hinglish or casual mixed language for WhatsApp
- Understanding a letter or notice written in a language you read slowly
- Creating bilingual content, like an English-and-Tamil poster text

## Translation versus re-writing

A literal translation often sounds stiff. Ask for natural, local phrasing instead:

```text
Translate this message into simple, natural Hindi as a shopkeeper would speak to regular customers. Do not translate word for word — keep the meaning and make it sound friendly.

"Dear customers, our shop will remain closed on Sunday for stock-taking. We will reopen on Monday at 10 am. Thank you for your support."
```

Compare that to just "Translate into Hindi". The first gives you something your customers would actually say; the second may give you textbook-style Hindi.

### Choosing the right register

Hindi and most Indian languages have different levels of formality. Be specific:

| What you want | How to ask |
|---|---|
| Formal / official | "Formal Hindi suitable for a government office letter" |
| Polite everyday | "Polite, simple Hindi using 'aap'" |
| Casual | "Casual Hindi, like a message between friends" |
| Hinglish | "Hinglish in Roman script, the way young professionals text" |
| Simple for all readers | "Very simple words, suitable for readers with basic schooling" |

## Script matters

Tell the assistant which script you want:

- **Devanagari** for Hindi or Marathi written traditionally: "Write in Hindi using Devanagari script."
- **Roman script** if the reader types on an English keyboard: "Write in Hindi but using English letters (Roman script)."
- For other languages, ask for the native script — Tamil, Bengali, Gujarati and so on.

For WhatsApp messages to customers, Roman-script Hinglish is often more familiar. For printed notices, posters or official letters, the native script usually looks more professional.

## Bilingual drafting

Small businesses often need the same message in two languages. Ask for both together:

```text
Write a short notice for our clinic in Coimbatore, in both English and Tamil, informing patients that Dr. Lakshmi will not be available from 10 to 14 November and that Dr. Arun will see patients instead. Keep each version under 50 words. Show the English first, then the Tamil.
```

## Understanding documents in another language

Received a notice in Marathi or a letter in Bengali that you read slowly? Paste it in (or upload a clear photo) and ask:

```text
Explain this notice in simple English. Then list any dates, amounts or actions I need to take.
```

This is very helpful for society notices, school circulars and local government communication. For anything legal or official, confirm the key details with someone fluent or with the issuing office.

## Accuracy checks for Indian languages

AI translation in Indian languages can go wrong in specific ways:

- **Unnatural word choices** — technically correct but nobody talks like that
- **Wrong gender agreement** in Hindi verbs and adjectives
- **Mixed-up honorifics** — using "tum" where "aap" is needed
- **Rare words** that your audience won't understand
- **Numbers and dates** that get shifted during translation

A good habit: ask the assistant to translate its output *back* into English. If the back-translation says something different from what you meant, the translation needs fixing.

```text
Now translate your Hindi version back into English, word for word, so I can check the meaning is correct.
```

And if the message is going to many people — a poster, a printed notice, an official letter — have a fluent speaker read it once before publishing.

## Voice input: speak in your language

Most assistant mobile apps let you speak instead of type. You can speak in Hindi or another language and ask the assistant to respond in English, or the reverse. This is useful for people who think faster in their mother tongue. Sunita, who runs a tailoring unit in Lucknow, dictates her notes in Hindi and asks the assistant to convert them into a neat English email to her fabric supplier.

## Try it yourself

Spend 15-20 minutes on a real multilingual task.

1. Choose an Indian language you read well, other than English.
2. Write a short English message you might really send: a festival greeting to customers, a notice to your housing society, or a reminder to a team.
3. Ask an assistant to write it in that language in two registers — formal and casual — using the prompts above.
4. Ask for a back-translation into English and check the meaning.
5. Mark any words that sound unnatural to you and ask the assistant to replace them with more common words.
6. Save your best prompt as Prompt #3 in your library, noting which language and register worked.

## Key takeaways

- AI assistants can draft and translate in Hindi and many Indian languages, with quality varying by language and tool.
- Ask for natural, local phrasing rather than word-for-word translation, and specify register and script.
- Bilingual messages can be produced together in one prompt.
- Use back-translation to check meaning, and have a fluent speaker review anything public or official.
- Voice input lets you think in your own language and get polished output in another.
