Throughout this course you've been saving prompts. Now it's time to turn them into a proper personal prompt library — 20 tested prompts you can reuse in seconds. This single habit is what separates occasional AI users from people who save hours every week.

## Why a prompt library matters

Without a library, every task starts from a blank chat box, and you re-invent the same instructions again and again. With a library:

- You get consistent, high-quality results because your prompts are already tested.
- You save time — copy, fill in the blanks, send.
- You can share prompts with colleagues and build team standards.
- You keep improving prompts over time instead of starting over.

Think of it like a kitchen's masala dabba: the right ingredients, ready to hand, every day.

## Where to keep your library

Pick whatever you'll actually open:

| Option | Good for |
|---|---|
| Google Docs or Word document | Simple, searchable, easy to share |
| Notes app (phone or desktop) | Quick access on the go |
| Spreadsheet | Sorting by category, tracking ratings |
| Notion or similar tools | Tagging, databases, team sharing |
| Built-in features | Many assistants offer custom instructions, saved prompts, or custom assistants/projects you can set up once |

A spreadsheet works well for beginners. Use columns: **#, Name, Category, Prompt, When to use, Tool that works best, Rating (1-5), Last updated.**

## The anatomy of a reusable prompt

A good library prompt has placeholders in square brackets, so you can fill in the specifics each time:

```text
NAME: Vendor follow-up email
CATEGORY: Email
PROMPT:
Act as a professional procurement executive.
Write a follow-up email to [vendor name] about [order/issue].
Background: [what happened, dates, order numbers].
What I need: [specific action and deadline].
Tone: [polite / firm / urgent].
Length: under [number] words, with a clear subject line.
Do not make any promises about [payments/penalties] unless I mention them.
WHEN TO USE: Delayed deliveries, missing documents, pending quotations.
```

Notice how it bakes in the lessons from this course: role, context, format, tone and a guardrail against unwanted promises.

## Organising 20 prompts by category

Aim for a spread that matches your real week. Here's a suggested structure — adjust to your job:

### Writing and communication (5)
1. Notes-to-email converter
2. Three-tone rewrite (warm / neutral / firm)
3. Polite "no" or scope-change message
4. Regional-language or Hinglish message with back-translation
5. Payment or task follow-up sequence

### Documents and research (5)
6. Report brief and outline
7. "Cut 20%" editor
8. Purpose-driven PDF summary with page numbers
9. Document Q&A with exact quotes
10. Research synthesis "using ONLY these sources"

### Data and meetings (4)
11. Excel formula from column description
12. Formula or error explainer
13. Rough notes to clean minutes with action items
14. Meeting agenda and talking points

### Career (3)
15. Job description decoder
16. Resume bullet impact rewriter (with [ADD NUMBER])
17. Mock interview, one question at a time

### Thinking and productivity (3)
18. "Ask me 5 questions first" starter
19. Critical reviewer: "List the 3 weakest points in this"
20. Daily planner: turn a messy to-do list into a prioritised plan

If you run a business, swap some for business prompts — Instagram captions, product descriptions, customer replies, GST invoice reminders. If you're a fresher, add prompts for learning new topics and preparing for assessments.

## Two powerful "meta" prompts

These two belong in every library.

**The prompt improver:**

```text
Here is a prompt I use often: [paste prompt]. Improve it so it gives more consistent, useful results. Add placeholders in [brackets] for anything that changes each time. Explain what you changed and why.
```

**The prompt maker:**

```text
I regularly need to [describe task]. Ask me questions about this task one at a time, then write a reusable prompt template with placeholders that I can save in my prompt library.
```

These let your library grow on its own.

## Testing and rating prompts

A prompt earns its place by working repeatedly. For each new prompt:

1. Test it on at least two real tasks.
2. Rate the output from 1 to 5.
3. If it's below 4, use the prompt improver and test again.
4. Note which assistant worked best for it.

Kiran, an admin officer at a Nashik engineering college, built a library of 25 prompts for circulars, parent emails and event reports. She shared the best ten with her team in a shared Google Doc; now new staff get up to speed much faster.

## Using built-in features

Most assistants let you save standing instructions so you don't repeat yourself — often called custom instructions, personalisation, or projects. Useful things to put there:

```text
I work as an operations executive at a logistics company in Pune. I write mostly to vendors and internal teams. Prefer simple, clear Indian English. Keep answers concise unless I ask for detail. Use ₹ for currency and Indian number formats (lakh, crore).
```

This improves every answer without any extra effort.

## Key takeaways

- A prompt library turns one-off experiments into reusable, reliable tools.
- Store prompts where you'll actually open them, with placeholders, categories and "when to use" notes.
- Aim for 20 prompts spread across writing, documents, data, meetings, career and productivity.
- Use the prompt improver and prompt maker to grow and refine your library.
- Save standing instructions about your role and preferences in your assistant's settings.
