Tender documents, annual reports, policy circulars, contracts, research papers — long PDFs are part of almost every job. In this lesson you'll learn to get accurate summaries and answers from long documents, and how to catch the details AI sometimes misses.

## Getting your document into the assistant

Most AI assistants let you upload a PDF, Word file or other document directly — look for a paperclip or "+" icon next to the message box. You can also paste text if the document is short.

A few practical points:

- **Scanned PDFs** (photos of pages) are harder. Many assistants can read them, but accuracy drops with poor scans, handwriting or tables. If possible, use a text-based PDF.
- **Very long documents** may exceed what the assistant can handle well in one go. If answers become vague, split the document into parts.
- **Confidential documents** — check your company's AI policy before uploading anything sensitive (see Module 5).

## Don't just say "summarise this"

"Summarise this" leaves the assistant guessing what you care about. A better prompt states your purpose:

```text
I am a purchase manager evaluating this tender document for our company, which supplies office furniture. Summarise it for me in this structure:
1. What is being procured (2 lines)
2. Eligibility criteria (bullets)
3. Key dates and deadlines (table)
4. Documents we must submit (checklist)
5. Penalty and payment terms
6. Anything unusual or risky that I should read in full
Mention page numbers wherever possible.
```

The purpose ("supplies office furniture") lets the assistant focus on what matters to *you*. Asking for page numbers makes checking much easier.

## Layered summaries

For big documents, work in layers:

1. **Overview first** — "In 5 bullets, what is this document about and who is it for?"
2. **Section map** — "List the main sections with one line on each."
3. **Deep dive** — "Now explain section 4 on payment terms in detail."
4. **Questions** — ask specific things you need to know.

This mirrors how an expert reads: skim, map, then focus.

## Asking questions of the document

Often you don't need a summary at all — you need answers:

- `What is the notice period for termination in this contract, and which clause says so?`
- `Does this policy cover pre-existing conditions? Quote the exact sentence.`
- `List every date mentioned in this document, with what happens on each.`
- `Compare what this document says about late payments with the clause on interest.`

The phrase **"quote the exact sentence"** is one of the most useful in this course. It forces the assistant to point to real text, which you can then find and confirm.

## Fact-checking a document summary

AI summaries of long documents can go wrong in quiet ways:

| Problem | Example | How to catch it |
|---|---|---|
| Missed detail | Skips a penalty clause on page 37 | Ask "What penalties or fines are mentioned anywhere?" |
| Blended facts | Mixes up two different deadlines | Ask for a table of all dates with page numbers |
| Invented content | States a clause that isn't there | Ask for exact quotes; search the PDF yourself (Ctrl+F) |
| Lost nuance | "Must" becomes "should" | Read the original wording for critical clauses |
| Number errors | ₹2.5 lakh becomes ₹25 lakh | Verify every amount in the source |

A practical rule: **anything you will act on, sign or repeat to someone else, find in the original PDF yourself.** Use Ctrl+F (or Cmd+F on Mac) to jump to the right place.

## Summaries for different audiences

The same document can be summarised differently for different readers:

```text
Using this RBI circular, write three summaries:
(a) 3 lines for our CEO — what changes and what it means for us
(b) a checklist for our compliance team
(c) a simple explanation for our branch staff, in plain language
```

Ritu, an associate at a Chennai CA firm, uses this approach when new notifications come out: one version for partners, one checklist for the audit team, and one client-friendly explanation she can adapt for emails.

## Comparing two documents

Need to see what changed between an old and new version of a policy, or compare two vendor quotations? Upload both and ask:

```text
Compare these two versions of our leave policy. List every difference in a table: topic, old version, new version. Do not include anything that is the same in both.
```

Then verify the important differences in the actual documents.

## Turning a document into action

After you understand a document, ask the assistant to help you act on it:

- `Create a checklist of everything we need to do to comply with this, with suggested owners.`
- `Draft an email to my team explaining the 3 most important changes in this policy.`
- `What questions should I ask the vendor before signing this agreement?`

## Try it yourself

Spend about 20 minutes on a real document.

1. Find a long PDF you actually need to understand: an annual report, a policy, an insurance document, a government scheme guideline or a contract (avoid confidential work documents unless your company allows it).
2. Upload it to an assistant and use a purpose-driven summary prompt with a clear structure and page numbers.
3. Ask three specific questions, each with "quote the exact sentence".
4. Verify all three answers in the PDF yourself using Ctrl+F. Note whether any were wrong or incomplete.
5. Ask for a summary for a different audience (for example, a family member or a junior colleague).
6. Save your best summary prompt as Prompt #6 in your library.

## Key takeaways

- Upload documents directly, but be careful with scanned files, very long files and confidential material.
- State your purpose and the structure you want instead of just "summarise this".
- Work in layers: overview, section map, deep dive, specific questions.
- Ask for page numbers and exact quotes, then verify anything important in the original.
- Tailor summaries to different audiences and turn understanding into checklists and actions.
