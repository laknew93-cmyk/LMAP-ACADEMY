AI assistants are powerful, but what you type into them matters. One careless paste of client data can create serious trouble for you and your employer. This lesson gives you simple, practical rules for using AI safely at work and at home.

## Why privacy matters with AI

When you type into an AI assistant, your text is sent to the company that runs it. Depending on the product, your account type and your settings, conversations may be stored, reviewed for safety, or used to improve the service. Business and enterprise versions generally come with stronger data protections, set by agreements between the vendor and your employer.

You don't need to be fearful — you need to be deliberate. Know what you're sharing, with which tool, under which account.

## What never goes into an unapproved AI tool

Treat these as off-limits unless your company has explicitly approved a tool for them:

- **Personal identifiers** — Aadhaar numbers, PAN, passport numbers, bank account and card details
- **Passwords, OTPs, API keys** — never, in any tool
- **Customer and employee data** — names with phone numbers, addresses, salaries, health information
- **Confidential business information** — unreleased financials, pricing strategy, M&A plans, legal disputes
- **Client documents** covered by confidentiality agreements
- **Source code or internal systems information** where your company restricts it

India's data protection law (the Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023) places responsibilities on organisations handling personal data. Your company's policy is how it meets those duties — so following the policy protects both you and your employer.

## The anonymise-then-ask technique

Most tasks don't actually need the sensitive details. Replace them before pasting:

| Instead of | Use |
|---|---|
| Mr. Ramesh Iyer, +91 98xxxxxx | Client A |
| Sundaram Textiles Pvt Ltd | Company X |
| ₹3,47,82,000 contract value | a contract worth several crores (or a rounded figure, if needed) |
| Specific employee name in an HR issue | Employee 1 |

For example:

```text
A long-standing client (Company X) is unhappy that our deliverable was 2 weeks late due to an internal resourcing issue. Draft an apology email that takes responsibility, explains the new timeline, and offers a goodwill gesture. Don't make any financial promise.
```

You get the same quality of help without exposing anyone. Add the real names back in your email client.

## Understanding your company's AI policy

Many organisations now have an AI policy — sometimes called an "acceptable use" or "generative AI" policy. If yours does, read it. Look for answers to these questions:

1. **Which tools are approved?** Often one specific enterprise tool (for example, Copilot or Gemini under the company account).
2. **What data can go in?** Some policies allow internal data in approved tools but not client data.
3. **Do I need to disclose AI use?** Some teams require you to mention when content was AI-assisted, especially for client deliverables.
4. **Who reviews AI output?** Many policies require human review before anything goes to customers.
5. **What's forbidden?** For example, using AI for hiring decisions, legal advice or customer-facing chatbots without approval.

If your company has no policy, ask your manager or IT team before using AI with work data. A two-line email like "Is it okay if I use [tool] to summarise internal documents without client names?" protects you.

## Personal account settings to check

For your personal AI accounts, spend ten minutes in the settings:

- **Chat history / activity** — understand whether conversations are saved and how to delete them.
- **Training on your data** — many assistants offer a setting to stop your conversations being used to improve their models. Decide what you're comfortable with.
- **Memory features** — some assistants remember facts about you across chats. Review what's saved and delete anything you don't want kept.
- **Connected apps** — if you've connected email, drive or calendar, check what access you've granted.
- **Shared links** — if you share a chat via link, anyone with the link may see it.

Settings names and locations change, so look in each product's privacy or data-controls section.

## Beyond privacy: other workplace risks

### Accuracy and accountability

If AI-assisted work contains an error, *you* are accountable, not the tool. Lessons 1.2 and 3.1 covered verification — apply them strictly to anything going to clients, management or the public.

### Copyright and originality

Don't ask AI to copy someone else's content, and don't present heavily AI-generated work as entirely your own where your organisation or client expects otherwise. For marketing content, check that images and text don't imitate a competitor or a real person.

### Scams using AI

AI also helps scammers write convincing messages. Be extra careful with emails, WhatsApp messages or calls claiming to be your bank, a government office or your boss asking for urgent payments or OTPs. Verify through an official channel. Never share OTPs.

### Bias and fairness

AI can reflect biases from its training data. Be careful using it for decisions about people — hiring, performance reviews, loans — and always apply human judgement.

## A simple personal AI policy

Write your own three-line policy and keep it near your desk:

```text
1. I use [approved tool] for work and [personal tool] for personal tasks — never mixing them.
2. I never paste passwords, ID numbers, financial details or client names; I anonymise first.
3. I verify facts, numbers and names before anything AI-assisted leaves my hands.
```

## Try it yourself

Allow about 15 minutes.

1. Find out whether your workplace (or a past one) has an AI policy. If yes, read it and note the approved tools and data rules. If not, draft the two-line email to your manager or IT team.
2. Open the settings of your personal AI assistant. Check chat history, training preferences, memory and connected apps. Adjust anything you're not comfortable with.
3. Take a real work task that involves sensitive details. Rewrite the prompt using the anonymise-then-ask technique.
4. Write your own three-line personal AI policy.
5. Save your anonymised prompt as Prompt #14 in your library.

## Key takeaways

- What you type is sent to the AI provider; storage and use depend on product, account and settings.
- Never paste passwords, OTPs, ID numbers, financial details or confidential client data into unapproved tools.
- Anonymise first — most tasks don't need real names or figures.
- Read your company's AI policy; if none exists, ask before using AI with work data.
- You remain accountable for accuracy, originality and fairness of AI-assisted work.
