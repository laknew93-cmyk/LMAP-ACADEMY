AI assistants can turn hours of research into minutes — but only if you know how to check what they tell you. In this lesson you'll learn a research workflow that combines AI speed with human verification, and you'll produce a one-page research brief from three sources.

## Two kinds of AI research

When you ask an assistant a research question, it answers in one of two ways:

1. **From memory** — it generates an answer from what it learned in training. Fast, but may be outdated or wrong, and it usually can't tell you exactly where the information came from.
2. **With web search** — it searches the internet, reads pages and summarises them, usually with links. Slower, but more current and checkable.

Most major assistants can search the web, and many show a "searching" indicator plus source links. For anything where accuracy or recency matters, make sure search is being used. You can ask directly:

```text
Search the web for this and give me links to your sources for every key point.
```

Some assistants also offer a "deep research" style mode that reads many sources and produces a longer report. These are useful for bigger questions, but the same verification rules apply.

## The research workflow: Explore, Gather, Verify, Synthesise

### 1. Explore — understand the landscape

Use the assistant to get oriented quickly:

```text
I'm a marketing executive at a mid-sized FMCG company in India. I need to understand the quick-commerce market (10-minute grocery delivery) for an internal presentation. Give me: the main players, how the business model works, 5 key questions I should research, and the types of sources I should look for.
```

This gives you a map, not final facts. Treat every specific number in this answer as unconfirmed.

### 2. Gather — collect real sources

Now find actual sources. Good source types in India include:

- Official government sites (ministries, RBI, SEBI, census and statistics portals)
- Company annual reports and investor presentations
- Reputed business newspapers and magazines
- Industry body reports
- Academic papers

Ask the assistant to help you find them, then open each link yourself. Check the page exists, says what the AI claims, and is reasonably recent.

### 3. Verify — check the claims

For each important fact, confirm it in the original source. Watch for:

- **Broken or fake links** — the page doesn't exist or is unrelated
- **Misquoted numbers** — the source says ₹12,000 crore, the summary says ₹1,200 crore
- **Old data presented as current** — a 2019 figure described as "today"
- **Opinion presented as fact** — one analyst's prediction stated as certainty

A useful habit: when two sources disagree, note both figures and the dates, rather than picking one silently.

### 4. Synthesise — make sense of it

Once you have verified material, paste the relevant extracts into the assistant and ask it to combine them:

```text
Below are extracts from 3 sources I have checked. Using ONLY this information, write a one-page research brief with: a 3-line summary, key findings (bullets, each with its source number in brackets), points where sources disagree, and 3 open questions.

Source 1: [title, date, link] [paste extract]
Source 2: [title, date, link] [paste extract]
Source 3: [title, date, link] [paste extract]
```

The phrase "using ONLY this information" is crucial. It keeps the assistant grounded in your verified sources instead of adding its own unverified facts.

## Asking better research questions

Vague questions get vague answers. Compare:

- Weak: "Tell me about electric vehicles in India."
- Better: "What are the main reasons small fleet operators in Tier-2 Indian cities hesitate to switch to electric three-wheelers? Group the reasons into cost, infrastructure and operations, and suggest what sources would have data on each."

Also ask the assistant to argue the other side:

```text
What are the strongest arguments against this conclusion? What evidence would change it?
```

This protects you from building a one-sided brief.

## Research for everyday decisions

Research isn't only for work presentations. People use the same workflow for:

- Comparing two health insurance policies (verify every clause in the actual policy document)
- Understanding a new government scheme for small businesses (confirm on the official portal)
- Choosing between courses or colleges for a family member
- Checking a potential employer before an interview

Meera, a career returner in Pune, used this approach to research three companies before her interviews: she asked an assistant for an overview, then checked each company's website, recent news and LinkedIn page herself. The AI saved her time; her checking made sure she didn't repeat outdated information in the interview.

## Citing AI-assisted research honestly

If you share a brief at work, cite the original sources, not "ChatGPT said". The assistant is your research helper, not your source. If your company has rules about disclosing AI use, follow them.

## Try it yourself — Project: one-page research brief

This is a course project. Allow about 20 minutes (more if you enjoy it).

1. Pick a question relevant to your work, business or job search. Example: "What should a small bakery know before selling on food delivery apps?"
2. **Explore**: ask an assistant for an overview, key questions and source types.
3. **Gather**: find three real sources. Open and skim each one yourself. Note title, date and link.
4. **Verify**: pick the three most important facts and confirm each in its original source.
5. **Synthesise**: paste extracts from your three sources into the "using ONLY this information" prompt.
6. Edit the brief to one page. Add a "Sources" list at the bottom.
7. Save the synthesis prompt as Prompt #5 in your library and keep the brief for your portfolio.

## Key takeaways

- Know whether the assistant is answering from memory or from a web search — use search for facts that matter.
- Follow Explore, Gather, Verify, Synthesise.
- Open every source yourself; check that links work and numbers match.
- Ground the final write-up with "using ONLY this information" and cite original sources.
- Ask for counter-arguments so your research isn't one-sided.
