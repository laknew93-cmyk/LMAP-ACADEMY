A carousel is a swipeable set of slides, usually uploaded to LinkedIn as a PDF document. Carousels are excellent for frameworks, checklists and step-by-step guides that people want to save. In this lesson you will plan, write and design a carousel with AI, and finish Project 3: one carousel plus three LinkedIn posts.

## When a carousel is the right format

Choose a carousel when your idea has a natural sequence or several parts:

- A process: "How to file your first freelance GST invoice in 6 steps"
- A comparison: "Old tax regime vs new regime: who should choose which"
- A checklist: "8 things to check before you accept a job offer"
- A framework you use with clients
- Common mistakes and their fixes

If the idea is a single opinion or story, a text post will usually work better.

## The anatomy of a good carousel

Most effective carousels run between 6 and 10 slides:

| Slide | Purpose | Tips |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Cover | Stop the scroll and promise value | Big, clear title; under 10 words; a specific benefit |
| 2. Problem or context | Why this matters | One relatable sentence or situation |
| 3–8. Content | One point per slide | Short headline plus one or two supporting lines |
| 9. Summary | Recap the key points | A compact list people can screenshot |
| 10. Close | Who you are and what to do next | Your name, one-line promise, a gentle call to action |

The golden rule is **one idea per slide**. If a slide needs more than about 30 words, split it. People read carousels on phones, so text must be large and spacing generous.

## Writing the carousel with AI

Start with your raw material: a process you actually follow, a checklist you give clients, or a list of mistakes you have seen. Then use a prompt like this:

```text
Using my voice guide, turn the notes below into a LinkedIn carousel
of 8 slides.
For each slide give:
- Slide number
- Headline (max 8 words)
- Body text (max 25 words)
- A simple visual suggestion (icon, diagram, or layout idea)
Slide 1 is a cover with a specific benefit-led title.
Slide 8 is a recap plus a soft call to action to follow me for more
on [topic].
Use only the information in my notes; do not invent numbers or claims.
NOTES:
[paste your process, checklist or framework]
```

Then ask for five alternative cover titles. The cover decides whether people swipe, so test options such as:

- "6 GST mistakes I see in every freelancer's first year"
- "Your first GST invoice, explained in 6 slides"
- "Freelancers: check these 6 things before you raise an invoice"

Edit the slide text yourself. Cut words ruthlessly. Make sure every claim is correct; if you are writing about tax, law or health, double-check against official sources, because errors in a saveable carousel spread.

## Designing the carousel

You do not need to be a designer. Most people use a design tool such as Canva, which has LinkedIn carousel templates, a document format sized for slides, and AI features that can help with layouts, text suggestions and image generation. Many presentation tools work too: set the slide size, design, then export as PDF.

Design principles that matter more than any tool:

- **Consistent template.** Same fonts, colours and layout on every slide. This becomes part of your brand.
- **Large text.** If you cannot read it comfortably on your phone held at arm's length, it is too small.
- **High contrast.** Dark text on a light background or the reverse. Avoid text over busy photos.
- **Slide numbers or a progress cue** so readers know how far they are.
- **Your name or handle** small on every slide, in case slides get screenshotted and shared.
- **Two brand colours and one or two fonts** at most.

You can ask a chat assistant to suggest a simple colour palette and font pairing that fits your voice guide, then build it once as a reusable template.

## Writing the accompanying post

A carousel still needs a text post above it. Keep it short: a hook that matches the cover, two or three lines on why you made it, and an invitation to save it. For example:

```text
Most freelancers learn GST the hard way: through a notice.

I made this 6-slide guide from the mistakes I've fixed for clients
over the last few years.

Save it for the next time you raise an invoice.
```

## Completing Project 3

Project 3 asks for one carousel and three LinkedIn posts. Aim for variety so you can learn what your audience prefers:

1. **The carousel**: a process, checklist or framework.
2. **A story post**: the one you drafted in the previous lesson.
3. **An opinion post**: a view you hold that some people in your field disagree with, explained respectfully.
4. **A how-to or lessons post**: practical, list-based, easy to save.

Use your content calendar to schedule them across two weeks rather than all at once.

## Try it yourself

Spend about 20 minutes finishing the drafts for Project 3.

1. Choose a process or checklist idea and write your raw notes.
2. Run the carousel prompt and five cover title options; edit the text to 8 slides.
3. Build the slides in Canva or a presentation tool using a consistent template, then export as PDF.
4. Write the short accompanying post.
5. Use the drafting workflow from Lesson 2.1 to write your opinion post and your how-to or lessons post.
6. Add all four items to your calendar with publishing dates and change their status to "ready".

## Key takeaways

- Use carousels for processes, checklists, comparisons and frameworks that people want to save.
- Keep to one idea per slide, around 6–10 slides, with large, high-contrast text.
- Let AI structure the slides and suggest covers, but write and fact-check the final text yourself.
- Build one reusable template so your carousels become recognisably yours.
- Pair each carousel with a short post and vary formats across the week to learn what works.
