You do not need twenty subscriptions to start. In this lesson you will set up a lean AI toolkit and, more importantly, a quality workflow — the habits that separate professionals from people who just paste chatbot output.

## The lean starter toolkit

Tools change quickly, so think in categories rather than brand names. Most have free tiers that are enough to begin with; upgrade only when a paid feature saves you real time on paid work.

| Category | What you use it for | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Chat assistant | Drafting, summarising, brainstorming, explaining | ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini |
| Design tool | Social posts, presentations, simple graphics | Canva, Google Slides |
| Docs and sheets | Writing, data cleaning, client reports | Google Workspace, Microsoft 365 |
| Transcription | Converting audio and video to text | Built-in transcription in many meeting and video tools |
| Automation | Connecting apps, routine workflows | Zapier, Make, Google Apps Script |
| Communication | Talking to clients | Email, WhatsApp Business, Google Meet |

Pick **one** chat assistant to learn deeply first. Knowing one tool well beats knowing five superficially.

## Writing prompts that produce usable work

A good prompt gives the assistant the same context you would give a new team member. Use this structure:

```text
Role: You are helping me, a freelance [service provider], with a client task.
Context: The client is [business type, location, audience].
Task: [Exactly what you need.]
Constraints: [Length, tone, language, words to avoid, format.]
Input: [Paste notes, brief or data here.]
Output: [Table / bullet list / 3 options / etc.]
```

Example for a Jaipur handicrafts store:

```text
Role: You are helping me, a freelance social media writer.
Context: The client is a family-run blue pottery store in Jaipur selling online
across India. Audience: women 25-45 who like handmade home decor.
Task: Write 5 Instagram captions for new tea-cup sets.
Constraints: Warm, simple English, max 60 words each, one emoji max,
no claims like "best in India", end with a soft call to action.
Input: Sets of 6 cups, hand-painted, microwave-unsafe, dispatch in 3 days.
Output: Numbered list.
```

Notice the detail "microwave-unsafe". If you leave out facts, the AI may invent them — for example, claiming the cups are dishwasher safe. Always give it the real facts.

## The quality workflow: draft, review, verify, own

Whatever service you offer, run every AI-assisted deliverable through these four steps.

### 1. Draft

Use AI to create the first version quickly. Treat it as raw material.

### 2. Review for fit

Read it as the client's customer would. Does it sound like this brand? Is it too generic? Remove clichés such as "in today's fast-paced world" and "unlock your potential". Add specifics only you or the client know: the shop's actual opening hours, the founder's real story, the local landmark.

### 3. Verify facts

AI tools can produce wrong numbers, invented sources, outdated rules and fake quotes, stated with total confidence. Check:

- Every number, date, price and statistic against a reliable source or the client.
- Every name, law, regulation or government scheme against an official source.
- Every quote — if you cannot find where it came from, remove it.
- For data work: spot-check rows, compare totals before and after, and keep the original file untouched.

### 4. Own it

Before sending, ask yourself: *Would I put my name on this if the client never knew AI was involved?* If not, it is not ready.

## Originality and copyright

Clients expect original work. A few practical rules:

- Do not ask AI to "rewrite" a competitor's article and pass it off as new. Use sources for understanding, then write from your own structure and the client's insights.
- Do not use images, music or fonts unless the licence allows commercial use. Design tools usually label what is free for commercial use; read those labels.
- If a client gives you material, confirm they have the right to use it.
- Many writers run important text through a plagiarism checker before delivery. It is a reasonable extra step for long-form content.

## Disclosing AI use

There is no single rule for every situation, but a good principle is: **disclose where it would matter to the client.** Some clients, publications and platforms have explicit policies on AI-generated content; always follow them. A simple line in your proposals works well:

```text
I use AI tools to speed up research and first drafts. Every deliverable is
reviewed, fact-checked and edited by me before it reaches you.
```

Most clients appreciate the honesty. It also protects you if questions arise later.

## Protecting client data

Be careful what you paste into AI tools. Customer phone numbers, Aadhaar or PAN details, bank information, medical records and confidential business plans should not go into a tool unless the client has agreed and you understand the tool's data settings. Anonymise data where you can — replace names with "Customer 1", "Customer 2" before asking for help with a spreadsheet.

## Try it yourself

Take 15–20 minutes.

1. Choose one task from your chosen service (e.g. five captions, a 300-word blog intro, cleaning a 20-row sample sheet).
2. Write a prompt using the Role–Context–Task–Constraints–Input–Output structure.
3. Generate the draft.
4. Run the four-step workflow. Use a highlighter or comments to mark: generic phrases you removed, facts you verified, and anything you corrected.
5. Count your changes. If there were none, look again — first drafts almost always need work.
6. Save both the prompt and your final version. Prompts that work well become templates later in the course.

## Key takeaways

- Start with one chat assistant, one design tool and your usual docs/sheets; add tools only when they pay for themselves.
- Detailed prompts with real facts reduce errors and generic output.
- Every deliverable goes through draft, review, verify and own.
- Respect originality and licences, disclose AI use where it matters, and never paste sensitive client data carelessly.
