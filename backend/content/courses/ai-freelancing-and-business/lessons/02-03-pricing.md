Pricing is the topic beginners worry about most. Charge too little and you burn out; charge too much without proof and nobody replies. This lesson gives you a practical method to set prices in ₹ and $ that you can defend, adjust and grow.

**Important:** every number in this lesson is an illustrative example to show the method. Rates vary hugely by city, client, skill, niche and platform. Nothing here is a promise or a guaranteed market rate.

## Three ways to price

### 1. Hourly pricing

You charge for time. Simple, but it has a problem in the AI era: if AI helps you finish faster, you earn less. Hourly works best for open-ended tasks like ongoing support or consulting calls.

### 2. Project pricing

You charge a fixed amount for a defined deliverable — "10 product descriptions" or "clean and deduplicate this customer list". Clients like knowing the cost upfront. You benefit from your own efficiency. The risk is underestimating effort, so define scope carefully.

### 3. Retainer pricing

A fixed monthly fee for an agreed set of ongoing work — for example, 12 social posts and a monthly report. Retainers give you predictable income and the client predictable support.

Most freelancers starting out use **project pricing** for one-time work and **retainers** for recurring work.

## Calculate your floor price

Before looking at the market, work out the minimum you need. This is your floor — the price below which a project is not worth taking.

```text
Monthly target income (example):         ₹30,000
Monthly costs (internet, tools, etc.):   ₹2,000
Total needed:                            ₹32,000
Billable hours available per month:      60
Floor hourly rate:                       ₹32,000 / 60 ≈ ₹533 per hour
```

Remember that not every working hour is billable. Outreach, learning, admin and invoicing take time too. Many freelancers find only half to two-thirds of their working hours are billable.

To convert to a project price: estimate hours, multiply by your rate, then add a buffer of 20–30% for revisions and communication.

## Check the market

Look at what others offer for similar work:

- Browse Fiverr and Upwork listings for your service and note the range.
- Look at LinkedIn posts from freelancers in your niche who mention their packages.
- Ask peers in freelancer communities.

You will see a wide spread. That is normal. Price reflects niche, proof and communication — not just the task.

The table below shows *example* ranges a beginner might consider when starting out. Treat them as a starting point for your own research, not as market data.

|---|---|---|
| 1,000-word blog post, researched and edited | ₹1,500 – ₹5,000 | $40 – $150 |

Your actual pricing will depend on your skills, proof and the client's budget. Some clients will pay more; many beginners start lower to win early projects. Platform fees and payment charges also reduce what you receive, so check current fees before you quote.

## Pricing for Indian vs international clients

You do not need one price for everyone. Many freelancers keep separate rate cards:

- **Indian small businesses** often have tight budgets and prefer clear, all-inclusive packages, frequently paying via UPI or bank transfer.
- **International clients** may have larger budgets but also more competition, time-zone challenges, and higher expectations on communication. Payment fees and currency conversion apply.

Quote international clients in their currency (usually USD) to avoid confusion.

## Present prices as packages

Offering three tiers helps clients choose rather than just decide yes or no:

```text
Basic   — 8 posts per month, captions + designs
Standard — 12 posts per month, captions + designs + content calendar
Premium — 12 posts + 4 reels scripts + monthly performance summary
```

Make the middle option the one you most want to sell.

## Handling "Can you do it cheaper?"

Instead of dropping your price, adjust the scope:

```text
I understand budgets are tight. For ₹6,000 I can do 8 posts instead of 12,
with the same quality and review process. Would that work for you?
```

This protects your rate while still helping the client.

## When to raise prices

Signals that it may be time: you are consistently busy, clients accept quotes without hesitation, or you now have testimonials and results to show. Raise prices for new clients first; give existing clients notice before changing theirs.

## Using AI to help with pricing

```text
I offer [service] to [niche] in India. My floor rate is ₹[X]/hour.
This package includes: [list]. Estimate hours for each step, add a
25% buffer, and suggest a project price. Then suggest Basic, Standard
and Premium tiers. Show your calculation in a table.
```

Treat the output as a calculator, not a market authority. You know your speed and your clients better.

## Try it yourself

About 15 minutes.

1. Calculate your floor hourly rate using your own numbers.
2. List every step in your core offer from Lesson 3 and estimate hours for each.
3. Calculate a project price with a 25% buffer.
4. Browse at least five similar listings on a freelance platform and note the range.
5. Create three tiers and fill in the "Price" section of your one-pager. Add a note: "Prices reviewed every 3 months."

## Key takeaways

- Use project pricing for one-time work and retainers for recurring work; hourly suits open-ended tasks.
- Calculate a floor price from your real costs and available billable hours.
- Market ranges vary widely; all figures here are illustrative examples, not promises.
- Offer tiered packages and adjust scope rather than cutting price.
