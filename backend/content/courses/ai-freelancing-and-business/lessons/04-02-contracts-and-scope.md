A contract is not about distrust; it is about clarity. Even a simple one-page agreement prevents most freelancing disputes — unpaid invoices, endless revisions, and arguments about who owns the work. This lesson covers what to include and how to use AI to draft one, with a clear reminder of where professional advice is needed.

**Note:** This lesson gives general information, not legal advice. For contracts involving large amounts, international clients, or unusual terms, consult a qualified lawyer.

## When do you need a contract?

For any paid work, have something in writing. Depending on the size of the project, this can be:

- **A confirmation message or email** for very small jobs (e.g. a ₹2,000 one-off task), listing deliverables, price, deadline and payment terms, with the client replying "agreed".
- **A one- to two-page service agreement** for larger projects or monthly retainers.
- **The platform's own terms** on Upwork or Fiverr, which govern work done through them. Keeping work and payment on the platform gives you its protections.

## What a simple service agreement includes

| Section | What it covers |
|---|---|
| Parties | Your name and the client's business name and address |
| Scope of work | Exactly what you'll deliver, with quantities |
| Exclusions | What is not included |
| Timeline | Start date, milestones, delivery dates |
| Client responsibilities | Inputs they must provide and by when |
| Fees and payment terms | Amount, advance, due dates, payment method |
| Revisions | Number of revision rounds included; rate for extra |
| Intellectual property | When ownership transfers (usually on full payment) |
| Confidentiality | How you'll protect their information |
| AI use | How you use AI tools and review output |
| Portfolio rights | Whether you can show the work publicly |
| Termination | How either side can end the agreement and what's paid |
| Signatures | Name, date, signature (digital is fine) |

## Key clauses explained

### Advance payment

Asking for an advance is common and reasonable, especially with new clients. Many freelancers ask for 30–50% upfront for projects and the first month in advance for retainers. This reduces the risk of doing work that never gets paid for.

### Revisions

Define a number: "Two rounds of revisions are included. Additional revisions are charged at ₹[X] per hour." Define what counts as a revision (changes to the delivered work) versus new work (a different topic, extra pages).

### Intellectual property

A common approach: the client owns the final deliverables once full payment is received. You keep ownership of your general templates, processes and know-how. Make this explicit so there's no confusion.

### AI use clause

A simple, transparent clause:

```text
The Freelancer may use AI tools to assist with research, drafting and
production. All deliverables are reviewed, edited and quality-checked by
the Freelancer. The Freelancer will not input the Client's confidential
information into third-party tools without the Client's consent.
```

Some clients may restrict AI use. Respect that, and adjust your pricing if it means more manual work.

### Late payment

State the due date (e.g. "within 7 days of invoice") and what happens if payment is late — for example, pausing work until dues are cleared.

## Drafting with AI

AI can produce a solid first draft, which you then review carefully.

```text
Draft a simple freelance service agreement under Indian context for:
- Freelancer: [your name], [city]
- Client: [business name], [city]
- Service: [description, quantities]
- Fee: ₹[amount], 50% advance, balance on delivery, within 7 days of invoice
- Revisions: 2 rounds included
- IP transfers on full payment; freelancer may show work in portfolio
- Include clauses for confidentiality, AI use with human review,
  termination with 7 days' notice, and payment for work completed.
Use plain English, numbered clauses, under 900 words.
Add a note at the top recommending review by a lawyer.
```

Then read every clause. Ask yourself: *Do I understand this? Is it fair to both sides? Can I actually keep this promise?* Remove anything you cannot explain. For significant contracts, have a lawyer review your template once — then you can reuse it.

## Managing scope creep

Scope creep is when a project gradually grows beyond what was agreed — "Can you also just do a quick logo?" A few strategies:

- Refer back to the agreement politely.
- Offer a quote for the extra work as a separate item.
- For good long-term clients, sometimes doing a small extra is a goodwill gesture — but choose it consciously and mention it: "Happy to include this one at no charge."

```text
Great idea! That's outside our current scope, so I've added it as an
optional extra below. Let me know if you'd like me to go ahead.
```

## Signing and storing

- Use a PDF with typed or drawn signatures, or a simple e-signature tool.
- Store signed copies in an organised folder: `Clients/ClientName/Contract.pdf`.
- Keep all important agreements in writing — email or a WhatsApp message both create a record. Avoid relying on phone calls alone for changes.

## Try it yourself

About 20 minutes.

1. Run the drafting prompt with details for your core offer.
2. Read the draft and highlight every clause you don't fully understand. Look each one up or simplify it.
3. Add a scope section that matches your one-pager's inclusions and exclusions.
4. Write a short "small job confirmation" template for tiny projects that don't need a full agreement.
5. Save both as templates in your "Delivery kit" folder. Note any clauses you want a lawyer to review.

## Key takeaways

- Put every paid project in writing, from a confirmation message to a full agreement.
- Cover scope, exclusions, timeline, fees, advance, revisions, IP, confidentiality, AI use and termination.
- AI can draft a contract; you must understand every clause, and a lawyer should review important templates.
- Handle scope creep by referring to the agreement and quoting extras separately.
